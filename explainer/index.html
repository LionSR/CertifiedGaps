The spacetime bootstrap

A proof that the spin-1 Heisenberg ring has a spectral gap, in nineteen figures, with an open-chain sequel

A quantum spin chain has discrete energy levels, and its spectral gap \(\Delta\) is the energy difference between the ground state and the first excited state. On a periodic ring of \(L\) spin-1 sites, this gap \(\Delta_L\) changes with the ring length \(L\); we want to prove that for every even \(L\) above a fixed length, \(\Delta_L\) stays above a positive constant.

Direct diagonalization can compute \(\Delta_L\) only on small rings, so the proof studies the ring at low temperature (large inverse temperature \(\beta\)) instead. At inverse temperature \(\beta\), Boltzmann weights \(e^{-\beta E}\) suppress excited states relative to the ground state like \(e^{-\beta\Delta_L}\), turning the spectral gap into an exponential decay rate in \(\beta\). To bound that decay rate from partition functions \(Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H_L}\), the spacetime bootstrap tracks how close a thermal state is to a single pure state through its purity \(P\in(0,1]\), or equivalently its purity defect \(1-P\). Once the defect is small at one finite seed scale \((n_0,\beta_0)\), doubling the ring length \(n\) and inverse temperature \(\beta\) together squares the defect at every step. The proof rests on three claims:

  • (S)The transfer operator is symmetric. Contracting \(Z_L(\beta)\) site by site defines a transfer operator \(X_\beta\) with \(Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}\). For the spin-1 Heisenberg bond, \(X_\beta\) is real symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real (Sections 6–7 and 12–14).
  • (D)Doubling squares the defects. If the spatial purity \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)\) and the thermal purity \(P_\tau(2n,\beta)\) are both at least \(1-\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\le1/100\), then the same two purities at \((2n,2\beta)\) are at least \(1-5\varepsilon^2\) (Sections 8–11 and 15). Claim (D) uses (S).
  • (I)The seed purities are at least \(0.99\). Both purities are at least \(0.99\) at \((n_0,\beta_0)=(2304,784)\) (Sections 17–18). Ref. [2] certifies this bound from exact traces on rings of at most \(12\) sites and one \(72\)-site matrix-product trial state, using (S) and six doubling steps from \((36,49/4)\).

Starting from (I) and applying (D) \(j\) times gives a defect of at most \(\varepsilon_j=20^{-2^j}/5\) at \(\beta_j=784\cdot2^j\), which Section 16 turns into \(\Delta_L>\log20/784\) for every even \(L\ge2304\). Before proving (S), (D), and (I), Sections 1–5 show why a gap is a decay rate in \(\beta\) and how the partition function \(Z_L(\beta)\) becomes a sum over histories on a spacetime torus.

1What is a gap?

Consider a periodic ring of \(L\) quantum spins (where site \(L+1\) is identified with site \(1\)), interacting with their nearest neighbors through the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg Hamiltonian \(H_L=\sum_{j=1}^L\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\). Here \(\mathbf S_j=(S_j^x,S_j^y,S_j^z)\) are the three spin-1 operators on site \(j\). Because the ring is finite, \(H_L\) has discrete energy levels \(E_0(L)\le E_1(L)\le\cdots\). The lowest level \(E_0(L)\) is the ground-state energy, and the spectral gap \(\Delta_L=E_1(L)-E_0(L)\) is the energy step to the first excited level.

On any single finite ring, \(\Delta_L\) is a difference of matrix eigenvalues; the hard problem is to prove that \(\Delta_L\) does not shrink to zero as \(L\to\infty\). In 1983, Haldane predicted that while the spin-\(\tfrac12\) chain is gapless (\(\Delta_L\to0\)), the spin-1 chain keeps a positive gap as \(L\to\infty\), which numerical calculations place near \(\Delta\approx0.4105\). The theorem proved on this page establishes Haldane’s prediction for all even ring lengths \(L\ge2304\), with the explicit lower bound \(\Delta_L>\log20/784\approx0.0038\).

\[ H_L=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1},\qquad \Delta_L=E_1(L)-E_0(L). \]

Theorem: \(\Delta_L>\log 20/784\approx0.0038\) for every even \(L\ge2304\), and the ground state is unique.

2A gap is a decay rate

To bound \(\Delta_L=E_1(L)-E_0(L)\) on a long ring, we cannot diagonalize \(H_L\) directly: on \(L\) spin-1 sites, \(H_L\) is a \(3^L\times 3^L\) matrix. Instead of solving for individual eigenvalues, we place the ring in thermal equilibrium at inverse temperature \(\beta\) (where large \(\beta\) means low temperature) and read the gap from how fast excited states decay as \(\beta\) grows.

At inverse temperature \(\beta\), each energy level \(E_m\) carries the Boltzmann weight \(e^{-\beta E_m}\). These weights sum to the partition function \(Z_L(\beta)=\sum_m e^{-\beta E_m}=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H_L}\), so level \(m\) has thermal probability \(w_m=e^{-\beta E_m}/Z_L(\beta)\). In the ratio of the first two probabilities, the denominator \(Z_L(\beta)\) cancels, leaving \(w_1/w_0=e^{-\beta(E_1-E_0)}=e^{-\beta\Delta_L}\).

Taking logarithms gives \(\log(w_1/w_0)=-\beta\Delta_L\): on a logarithmic plot against \(\beta\), \(\log(w_1/w_0)\) is a straight line of slope \(-\Delta_L\). To prove \(\Delta_L\ge c\), it therefore suffices to show that \(w_1/w_0\le e^{-c\beta}\) with the same rate \(c>0\) for every ring length \(L\).

\[ \frac{w_1}{w_0}=e^{-\beta\Delta_L}\le e^{-c\beta}\ \text{ for all }\beta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta_L\ge c. \]

3Imaginary time

The thermal operator \(e^{-\beta H_L}\) in \(Z_L(\beta)\) isolates the ground state by evolving in imaginary time. To see how this evolution acts on a state, expand any state \(\psi\) in the energy eigenstates \(|m\rangle\) of \(H_L\) as \(\psi=\sum_m c_m|m\rangle\), where each complex amplitude \(c_m\) has magnitude \(|c_m|\) and probability \(|c_m|^2\).

In ordinary real time \(t\), quantum evolution \(e^{-iHt}\psi\) multiplies each amplitude \(c_m\) by the phase factor \(e^{-iE_mt}\). Viewed as an arrow in the complex plane, \(c_m\) rotates at speed \(E_m\) with fixed magnitude, so every probability \(|c_m|^2\) remains constant.

Replacing real time \(t\) by imaginary time \(t=-i\tau\) with \(\tau>0\) turns the oscillating phase \(e^{-iE_mt}\) into a real damping factor \(e^{-\tau E_m}\). Each amplitude now shrinks without rotating, and higher energies decay fastest. After normalizing the state so that the probabilities sum to \(1\), the ground state dominates and the first excited amplitude ratio \(|c_1(\tau)|/|c_0(\tau)|\) decays at rate \(e^{-\tau\Delta}\). At \(\tau=\beta\), the damping factors \(e^{-\beta E_m}\) are the Boltzmann weights of Section 2: long imaginary time is equivalent to low temperature.

\[ e^{-iHt}\psi=\sum_m c_me^{-iE_mt}|m\rangle\ \xrightarrow{\ t=-i\tau\ }\ e^{-\tau H}\psi=\sum_m c_me^{-\tau E_m}|m\rangle,\qquad \frac{|c_1(\tau)|}{|c_0(\tau)|}\propto e^{-\tau\Delta}. \]

4A sum over histories

To compute the partition function \(\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}\) without diagonalizing \(H\), we factor the imaginary-time interval \(\beta\) into \(M\) tiny steps of size \(d\tau=\beta/M\), writing \(e^{-\beta H}=A^M\) with single-step matrix \(A=e^{-d\tau H}\approx I-d\tau H\). The definition of matrix multiplication then turns the matrix power \(A^M\) into a sum over step-by-step histories.

For two matrices \(A\) and \(B\), the entry in row \(i\) and column \(k\) of their product is \((AB)_{ik}=\sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}\): to go from state \(i\) to state \(k\) in two steps, pass through an intermediate state \(j\), multiply the two step entries \(A_{ij}\) and \(B_{jk}\) used along that route, and add over every possible \(j\). For example, with \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\), the top-left entry of \(A^2\) sums two routes, \(1\to1\to1\) (weight \(2\times2=4\)) and \(1\to2\to1\) (weight \(1\times1=1\)), giving \((A^2)_{11}=4+1=5\). Repeating this rule across \(M\) factors, an entry \((A^M)_{ij}\) is a sum over all \(M\)-step paths \(i=s_0\to s_1\to\cdots\to s_M=j\), where each path is weighted by the product of the matrix entries it uses.

Apply this to a single spin with states \(|\!\uparrow\rangle,|\!\downarrow\rangle\) and Hamiltonian \(H=-\sigma^x=-\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\). For a tiny step \(d\tau\), the step matrix is \(A=e^{-d\tau H}\approx\begin{pmatrix}1&d\tau\\d\tau&1\end{pmatrix}\): staying in the same spin state has weight \(1\) (diagonal entries), while flipping to the opposite state has weight \(d\tau\) (off-diagonal entries). As \(d\tau\to0\), a path becomes a continuous-time world line on \([0,\beta]\) that stays constant except at \(k\) flip times. Each flip carries weight \(d\tau\), and choosing \(k\) ordered flip times in \([0,\beta]\) gives volume \(\beta^k/k!\). The trace \(\operatorname{Tr}(A^M)=\sum_i(A^M)_{ii}\) keeps only periodic paths that return to their starting state, so a path must flip an even number of times \(k\) from either of the \(2\) starting states (\(\uparrow\) or \(\downarrow\)). Summing \(2\beta^k/k!\) over even \(k\) gives \(2\cosh\beta\), the exact value of \(\operatorname{Tr}e^{\beta\sigma^x}\). This sum over continuous-time trajectories is the Euclidean path integral.

The same identity counts walks on a graph: (Aᴹ)ᵢⱼ with A the adjacency matrix.

\[ (A^M)_{ij}=\sum_{\text{paths }i\to j}\ \prod_{\text{steps}}A_{s_ts_{t+1}},\qquad \operatorname{Tr}e^{\beta\sigma^x}=\sum_{k\ \mathrm{even}}\frac{2\beta^k}{k!}=2\cosh\beta . \]

5The partition function is a torus

For a single spin, a history is a world line on the interval \([0,\beta]\). For a ring of \(L\) spins, laying the \(L\) sites horizontally along space and plotting imaginary time \(\tau\in[0,\beta]\) vertically forms an \(L\times\beta\) spacetime sheet.

Both pairs of opposite edges of this sheet are glued into circles. Vertically, factoring \(e^{-\beta H_L}=(e^{-\beta H_L/M})^M\) into \(M\) imaginary-time steps and taking the trace glues the final time slice \(\tau=\beta\) to the initial slice \(\tau=0\), forming a circle of circumference \(\beta\). Horizontally, the periodic boundary condition identifies site \(L+1\) with site \(1\), forming a circle of circumference \(L\).

With both space and imaginary time periodic, the partition function \(Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H_L}\) is a sum over histories on an \(L\times\beta\) spacetime torus. The proof works directly in the continuous-time limit (\(M\to\infty\)), so there is no Trotter discretization error.

\[ Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H_L}=\operatorname{Tr}\big(e^{-\beta H_L/M}\big)^M . \]

6Histories of a chain

To see how \(Z_L(\beta)\) depends on the ring length \(L\), we contract the spacetime torus one site at a time around the ring. First consider a classical Ising ring of \(L\) spins \(s_j=\pm1\), where each neighboring pair \((s_j,s_{j+1})\) contributes a bond weight \(T(s_j,s_{j+1})=e^{Ks_js_{j+1}}\) (\(e^K\) when neighbors agree, \(e^{-K}\) when they disagree). Draw the \(2\times2\) transfer matrix \(T\) as a box with two horizontal legs for its row and column spin indices. Joining the leg \(s_2\) shared by the first two boxes sums over \(s_2=\pm1\), which is matrix multiplication \(\sum_{s_2}T(s_1,s_2)T(s_2,s_3)=(T^2)(s_1,s_3)\). Joining all \(L\) boxes around the ring and closing the last leg back to the first traces the matrix product: \(Z=\operatorname{Tr}T^L\).

The quantum spin-1 chain follows the same pattern once the classical spin on each leg is replaced by a continuous-time bond history. To write the Heisenberg bond in real matrices, switch to the Cartesian spin-1 basis \(|x\rangle,|y\rangle,|z\rangle\) and define \(G_\alpha=iS^\alpha\) for \(\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}\). These are real antisymmetric \(3\times3\) matrices: for example, \(G_z=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\) maps \(|x\rangle\to-|y\rangle\) and \(|y\rangle\to|x\rangle\) while annihilating \(|z\rangle\), so \(G_zG_z=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,0)\) has trace \(-2\). Because \(G_{\alpha,j}\otimes G_{\alpha,j+1}=-S_j^\alpha\otimes S_{j+1}^\alpha\), each bond term in \(-H_L\) decomposes as \(-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}=\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}G_{\alpha,j}\otimes G_{\alpha,j+1}\).

With this decomposition, expanding \(e^{-\beta H_L}\approx(I-d\tau H_L)^M\) in steps \(d\tau\) means that each step either acts as the identity (weight \(1\)) or fires one bond term \(G_{\alpha,j}\otimes G_{\alpha,j+1}\) (weight \(d\tau\)). Each such action is a bond event: a rung across bond \((j,j+1)\) at an imaginary time \(\tau\in(0,\beta)\), labeled by \(\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}\), which places the real \(3\times3\) matrix \(G_\alpha\) on both site \(j\) and site \(j+1\) at time \(\tau\). The list of all events on a single bond during \(0<\tau<\beta\) is a bond history, which serves as the index passed from site to site along the ring.

Once the bond histories are fixed, operators on different sites commute, so the trace over the chain factors into one trace per site. Along a single site’s world line from \(|s'\rangle\) at \(\tau=0\) to \(\langle s|\) at \(\tau=\beta\), events from its left bond history \(a\) and its right bond history \(b\) multiply in chronological order (the time-ordering symbol \(\mathcal T_\tau\) places later imaginary times to the left) to form the local four-leg tensor \(W_\beta^{ss'}(a,b)\). Gluing \(\tau=\beta\) to \(\tau=0\) sets \(s=s'\) and sums over \(s\in\{x,y,z\}\), tracing that matrix product into the transfer kernel \(X_\beta(a,b)\): for example, no events gives \(\operatorname{Tr}I=3\); one \(z\)-event on each side gives \(\operatorname{Tr}G_zG_z=-2\); and \(a=\{z\text{ at }0.2,\,x\text{ at }0.6\}\) with \(b=\{y\text{ at }0.4\}\) gives \(\operatorname{Tr}(G_xG_yG_z)=1\). Contracting the shared bond-history legs around all \(L\) sites integrates over all bond histories, yielding \(Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}\).

Gα = iSα in the Cartesian basis |x⟩, |y⟩, |z⟩ are real antisymmetric 3 × 3 matrices.

\[ (G_\alpha)_{ss'}=\varepsilon_{\alpha ss'}:\qquad G_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad G_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-1\\0&0&0\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad G_z=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}. \]

7Two ways to slice the torus

We now have two ways to slice the same \(L\times\beta\) spacetime torus, and equating them connects the energy levels of the \(L\)-site ring to the eigenvalues of a single transfer operator \(X_\beta\) that does not depend on \(L\).

Slicing horizontally across space at fixed imaginary time propagates the \(L\)-spin state upward by \(e^{-\beta H_L}\), giving the thermal trace over energy eigenvalues \(Z_L(\beta)=\sum_m e^{-\beta E_m(L)}\). Slicing vertically along imaginary time isolates one site’s world line on \([0,\beta]\) and propagates bond histories around the ring via \(X_\beta\), giving \(Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}=\sum_i\lambda_i(\beta)^L\).

In the horizontal sum, the energy eigenvalues \(E_m(L)\) depend on \(L\); in the vertical sum, the transfer-operator eigenvalues \(\lambda_i(\beta)\) depend on \(\beta\) alone, and the ring length \(L\) enters only as an exponent. Just as large \(\beta\) makes the ground energy \(E_0\) dominate the horizontal sum, large \(L\) makes the leading eigenvalue \(\lambda_0(\beta)\) dominate the vertical sum.

\[ Z_L(\beta)=\sum_m e^{-\beta E_m}=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}=\sum_i\lambda_i(\beta)^L . \]

8Purity, seen as squares

To bound the gap from Section 2, we need the ground state to carry nearly all of the Boltzmann probability \(w_0\), leaving little room for the first excited probability \(w_1\). We can certify that a single largest weight \(p_0\) is close to \(1\) without computing individual weights by measuring the distribution’s purity.

For any probability list \(p_0\ge p_1\ge\cdots\ge0\) summing to \(1\) (or any positive density matrix with unit trace and eigenvalues \(p_i\)), the purity is \(P=\sum_i p_i^2\) and the purity defect is \(1-P\). Lay the probabilities end to end along a segment of length \(\sum_i p_i=1\) and draw a square of side \(p_i\) on each interval: the total area of the squares is \(P=\sum_i p_i^2\).

Because every square has height at most \(p_0\), all the squares fit inside a rectangle of width \(1\) and height \(p_0\). Thus \(P\le p_0\): the purity is a direct lower bound on the largest probability. A purity of \(0.99\) (defect \(0.01\)) guarantees that a single dominant state holds at least \(99\%\) of the weight, and all other states combined hold at most \(1\%\).

\[ P=\sum_ip_i^2\ \le\ p_0\sum_ip_i=p_0 . \]

9Two purities from one torus

The two ways of slicing the spacetime torus (Section 7) define two normalized states, and taking the purity of either state glues two identical tori into a single torus of twice the size.

Write \(Z_n(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,n}\) for the partition function of an \(n\)-site ring at inverse temperature \(\beta\). First, cut the torus vertically along imaginary time between two sites, opening one bond-history leg of the ring. Dividing \(X_\beta^{\,n}\) by \(Z_n(\beta)\) gives the normalized spatial state \(\sigma_n=X_\beta^{\,n}/Z_n(\beta)\), with weights \(p_i=\lambda_i(\beta)^n/Z_n(\beta)\). Its purity \(\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_n^2)\) joins two copies of \(\sigma_n\) leg to leg into a closed ring of \(2n\) sites: the spatial purity \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}(X_\beta^{\,2n})/Z_n(\beta)^2=Z_{2n}(\beta)/Z_n(\beta)^2\).

Second, cut the torus horizontally across all \(n\) sites at a fixed imaginary time \(\tau\). Dividing \(e^{-\beta H_n}\) by \(Z_n(\beta)\) gives the thermal Gibbs state \(\rho_n=e^{-\beta H_n}/Z_n(\beta)\), with Boltzmann weights \(w_m=e^{-\beta E_m(n)}/Z_n(\beta)\). Its purity \(\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_n^2)=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-2\beta H_n})/Z_n(\beta)^2\) stacks two copies vertically in imaginary time into a torus of height \(2\beta\): the thermal purity \(P_\tau(n,\beta)=Z_n(2\beta)/Z_n(\beta)^2\). Both purities are ratios of partition functions of equal spacetime area \(2n\beta\), so any constant energy shift per site cancels identically in both.

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)=\frac{Z_{2n}(\beta)}{Z_n(\beta)^2},\qquad P_\tau(n,\beta)=\frac{Z_n(2\beta)}{Z_n(\beta)^2}. \]

10Squaring sharpens

Gluing two copies of a torus together does more than measure the purity \(P=1-\varepsilon\): it produces the normalized state at the doubled scale, with squared weights and a quadratically smaller defect.

In space, gluing two copies of \(\sigma_n=X_\beta^{\,n}/Z_n(\beta)\) gives \(\sigma_n^2=X_\beta^{\,2n}/Z_n(\beta)^2=P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)\,\sigma_{2n}\), so \(\sigma_n^2/\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_n^2)=\sigma_{2n}\) is the spatial state on a ring of length \(2n\). In imaginary time, stacking two copies of \(\rho_n(\beta)\) gives \(\rho_n(\beta)^2=P_\tau(n,\beta)\,\rho_n(2\beta)\), the Gibbs state at inverse temperature \(2\beta\). In both cases, each weight \(p_i\) maps to \(p_i'=p_i^2/P\), suppressing subleading weights relative to \(p_0\).

11Space and time are tied

Squaring \(\rho_n(\beta)\) improves the thermal purity by doubling \(\beta\), but leaves the ring length \(n\) fixed. Conversely, at fixed \(\beta\), increasing \(n\) gives thermal excitations more room and lowers the purity. To keep the purity close to \(1\) on arbitrarily long rings, \(n\) and \(\beta\) must double together via an exact identity that links spatial and thermal purities.

Rearranging the definitions from Section 9 gives \(Z_{2n}(\beta)=P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)\,Z_n(\beta)^2\) for a side-by-side spatial gluing and \(Z_n(2\beta)=P_\tau(n,\beta)\,Z_n(\beta)^2\) for a vertical thermal stacking: gluing two identical tori multiplies the squared partition function by the corresponding purity.

Now assemble four identical \(n\times\beta\) tori, with product \(Z_n(\beta)^4\), into a single \(2n\times2\beta\) torus \(Z_{2n}(2\beta)\) in two orders. Gluing horizontally first multiplies by \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)^2\) to form two \(2n\times\beta\) tori \(Z_{2n}(\beta)^2\), and stacking those vertically multiplies by \(P_\tau(2n,\beta)\). Stacking vertically first multiplies by \(P_\tau(n,\beta)^2\) to form two \(n\times2\beta\) tori \(Z_n(2\beta)^2\), and gluing those horizontally multiplies by \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,2\beta)\). Both orders produce \(Z_{2n}(2\beta)\), so their purity products are equal. Since \(P_\tau(n,\beta)\le1\), dividing by \(P_\tau(n,\beta)^2\) converts a spatial bound at \(\beta\) and a thermal bound at \(2n\) into a spatial bound at \(2\beta\).

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n,2\beta)\,P_\tau(n,\beta)^2=\frac{Z_{2n}(2\beta)}{Z_n(\beta)^4}=P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)^2\,P_\tau(2n,\beta). \]

12Why the proof needs a symmetric transfer matrix

Sections 8–11 treated the weights \(p_i=\lambda_i^n/Z_n\) of the spatial state \(\sigma_n=X_\beta^{\,n}/Z_n\) as nonnegative numbers summing to \(1\). For these weights \(\lambda_i^n/Z_n\) to be nonnegative at every even ring length \(n\), the eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) of \(X_\beta\) must be real, which holds when \(X_\beta\) is symmetric.

A \(2\times2\) example shows what fails when \(X\) is not symmetric. For the symmetric matrix \(X=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.3\\0.3&0.5\end{pmatrix}\), the eigenvalues \(1.06\) and \(0.34\) are real, so for \(n=1,2,3,4,\dots\) the spatial purity \(Z_{2n}/Z_n^2=\operatorname{Tr}(X^{2n})/(\operatorname{Tr}X^n)^2\) takes the values \(0.63, 0.83, 0.94, 0.98, \dots\), staying in \((0,1]\) and converging to \(1\). For the non-symmetric matrix \(X=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&-0.4\\0.4&0.9\end{pmatrix}\) with complex eigenvalues \(0.9\pm0.4i\), \(\operatorname{Tr}X^n\) changes sign and \(Z_{2n}/Z_n^2\) takes the values \(0.40, -0.11, -4.2, -47\), violating both \(P_{\mathrm s}\le1\) and the squaring inequality. The bootstrap therefore requires \(X_\beta\) to be a self-adjoint (real symmetric) operator; this is claim (S), established in Sections 13 and 14.

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n)=\frac{Z_{2n}}{Z_n^2}=\sum_ip_i^2\in(0,1]\quad\text{only if}\quad p_i=\frac{\lambda_i^n}{Z_n}\ \text{are real and}\ \ge0 . \]

13Where the symmetry comes from

In Section 6, the transfer operator \(X_\beta(a,b)\) was defined as a kernel whose row index is the left bond history \(a\) and whose column index is the right bond history \(b\). Symmetry \(X_\beta(a,b)=X_\beta(b,a)\) therefore means that reflecting a site’s transfer tensor horizontally, swapping the histories arriving from its left and right bonds, leaves the site trace unchanged.

This left–right symmetry follows directly from the bond decomposition. In \(X_\beta(a,b)\), a site collects all events arriving from its left bond (\(a\)) and right bond (\(b\)) and multiplies their \(3\times3\) matrices in time order. For the Heisenberg antiferromagnet, \(-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}=\sum_\alpha G_\alpha\otimes G_\alpha\), so every event of type \(\alpha\) places the same real matrix \(G_\alpha\) on both ends of the bond. A site therefore receives the same matrix \(G_\alpha\) whether the event arrives from its left or right bond: with \(a=\{G_z\ \text{at}\ 0.2,\ G_x\ \text{at}\ 0.6\}\) and \(b=\{G_y\ \text{at}\ 0.4\}\), both \(X(a,b)\) and \(X(b,a)\) multiply the same three matrices at the same times and equal \(\operatorname{Tr}G_xG_yG_z=1\).

By contrast, a bond term that places different operators on its two ends breaks this left–right symmetry: with \(G_z\) on the left site of a bond and \(P=\mathrm{diag}(1,0,0)\) on the right site, two events give \(\operatorname{Tr}(P^2)=1\) from the left bond and \(\operatorname{Tr}(G_z^2)=-2\) from the right bond (or, with \(Q=-G_z^2=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,0)\) on the left and \(P\) on the right, \(\operatorname{Tr}(P^2)=1\) versus \(\operatorname{Tr}(Q^2)=2\)). In general, a nearest-neighbor bond interaction \(h\) gives a self-adjoint transfer operator whenever \(-h\) factors symmetrically across the bond as \(-h=\sum_\alpha C_\alpha\otimes\overline{C_\alpha}\) (with a real symmetric on-site term), a condition known as reflection positivity.

Formal statement of reflection-positive rings and transfer operators: lean/CertifiedGaps/Ring/Transfer.lean.

\[ -\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}=+\sum_\alpha G_\alpha\otimes G_\alpha\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X(a,b)=X(b,a)\in\mathbb R . \]

14Testing a bond for reflection positivity

To test whether a two-site bond term \(h\) has a reflection-positive decomposition \(-h=\sum_\alpha C_\alpha\otimes\overline{C_\alpha}\) without guessing the matrices \(C_\alpha\), write \(-h\) on two spin-1 sites in terms of its \(3^4=81\) matrix elements \(\langle ij|{-h}|kl\rangle\), where \(i,k\in\{x,y,z\}\) act on the left site and \(j,l\in\{x,y,z\}\) act on the right site. Usually these \(81\) entries are arranged into a \(9\times9\) matrix with row \((i,j)\) and column \((k,l)\).

Instead, regroup the same \(81\) entries by site into the \(9\times9\) realigned matrix \(R[-h]\) whose row index \((i,k)\) belongs to the left site and whose column index \((j,l)\) belongs to the right site: \(R[-h]_{(ik),(jl)}=\langle ij|{-h}|kl\rangle\). Writing \(\mathrm{vec}(A)\) for the \(9\)-component column vector of entries \(A_{ik}\), this bond realignment turns any tensor product \(A\otimes B\) into the rank-one outer product \(\mathrm{vec}(A)\,\mathrm{vec}(B)^{\mathsf T}\).

Under realignment, a reflection-positive decomposition \(-h=\sum_\alpha C_\alpha\otimes\overline{C_\alpha}\) becomes a sum of rank-one positive matrices \(R[-h]=\sum_\alpha v_\alpha v_\alpha^\dagger\) with \(v_\alpha=\mathrm{vec}(C_\alpha)\), so \(h\) is reflection positive if and only if \(R[-h]\) is Hermitian positive semidefinite (\(\succeq0\)). For the spin-1 Heisenberg antiferromagnet (\(h=\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\)), \(R[-h]=\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)\,\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)^{\mathsf T}\) has eigenvalues \(2, 2, 2\) and six zeros, confirming claim (S); the ferromagnet (\(h=-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\)) has eigenvalues \(-2, -2, -2\) and fails for any on-site shift.

Rule for bilinear chains: reflection positive exactly when an even number of couplings Jx, Jy, Jz are ferromagnetic.

\[ R\big[A\otimes B\big]=\mathrm{vec}(A)\,\mathrm{vec}(B)^{\mathsf T},\qquad R\big[-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\big]=\sum_\alpha\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)\,\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)^{\mathsf T}\succeq0 . \]

15One doubling step

With claim (S) in hand, the squaring bound of Section 10 and the coupling identity of Section 11 combine into claim (D), the single doubling step. Suppose at an even ring length \(n\) and inverse temperature \(\beta\) both the spatial purity \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)\) and the doubled-length thermal purity \(P_\tau(2n,\beta)\) are at least \(1-\varepsilon\), with defect \(\varepsilon\le1/100\). Four alternating steps, coupling, spatial squaring, coupling, and thermal squaring, propagate these bounds to the doubled scale \((2n,2\beta)\):

  1. Couple to double \(\beta\) in space: The coupling identity of Section 11 gives \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,2\beta)=P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)^2P_\tau(2n,\beta)/P_\tau(n,\beta)^2\). Since \(P_\tau(n,\beta)\le1\), dropping the denominator leaves \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,2\beta)\ge(1-\varepsilon)^3\ge1-3\varepsilon\), multiplying the defect by at most \(3\).
  2. Square in space to double \(n\): Because \(X_{2\beta}\) is symmetric and \(n\) is even, squaring \(\sigma_n(2\beta)\) to \(\sigma_{2n}(2\beta)\) applies the squaring map \(f\) of Section 10, sending the defect \(1-(1-\varepsilon)^3\le3\varepsilon\) to \(\delta=f(1-(1-\varepsilon)^3)\approx\tfrac92\varepsilon^2\le5\varepsilon^2\) (for \(\varepsilon\le1/100\)). Thus \(P_{\mathrm s}(2n,2\beta)\ge1-5\varepsilon^2\).
  3. Couple at length \(2n\): Applying the coupling identity at size \(2n\) gives \(P_\tau(4n,\beta)=P_\tau(2n,\beta)^2P_{\mathrm s}(2n,2\beta)/P_{\mathrm s}(2n,\beta)^2\ge(1-\varepsilon)^2(1-\delta)\ge(1-\varepsilon)^3\), a defect of at most \(3\varepsilon\).
  4. Square in time to double \(\beta\) at length \(4n\): Squaring the Gibbs state \(\rho_{4n}(\beta)\) to \(\rho_{4n}(2\beta)\) applies \(f\) once more, giving \(P_\tau(4n,2\beta)\ge1-\delta\ge1-5\varepsilon^2\).

The output pair at \((2n,2\beta)\) has the same form as the input pair at \((n,\beta)\), with \(\varepsilon\) replaced by \(5\varepsilon^2\), which proves claim (D). More generally, the exact map \(\varepsilon\mapsto\delta\) contracts whenever \(\varepsilon<\varepsilon_*\approx0.12731\).

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta),\,P_\tau(2n,\beta)\ge1-\varepsilon\ \Longrightarrow\ P_{\mathrm s}(2n,2\beta),\,P_\tau(4n,2\beta)\ge1-5\varepsilon^2 . \]

16Repeat, and read off the gap

Once the seed defect satisfies \(5\varepsilon_0<1\), repeating the doubling step drives the defect down doubly exponentially in the step index \(j\), and therefore exponentially in the inverse temperature \(\beta_j=2^j\beta_0\). Starting from claim (I), \(\varepsilon_0=1/100\) at \((n_0,\beta_0)=(2304,784)\), \(j\) iterations of \(\varepsilon_{j+1}=5\varepsilon_j^2\) give \(\varepsilon_j=20^{-2^j}/5\) at \((n_j,\beta_j)=(2304\cdot2^j,\,784\cdot2^j)\). Since \(2^j=\beta_j/784\), this bound equals \(\varepsilon_j=\tfrac15e^{-\beta_j\log20/784}\): on a log plot against \(\beta\), the points lie on a line of slope \(-\log20/784\).

These bounds hold at the doubled lengths \(n_j=2304\cdot2^j\). To cover every even ring length \(L\ge2304\), choose the scale \(j\) with \(n_j\le L\le2n_j\). For even lengths the normalized weights \(p_i=|\lambda_i|^n/Z_n\) lie in \([0,1]\) and sum to \(1\), so raising them to any power \(r\ge1\) gives \(Z_{rn}/Z_n^{\,r}=\sum_ip_i^r\le1\). Setting \(r=L/n_j\) at \(\beta_j\) yields \(Z_L(\beta_j)\le Z_{n_j}(\beta_j)^{L/n_j}\), and setting base length \(L\) and \(r=2n_j/L\) at \(2\beta_j\) yields \(Z_L(2\beta_j)\ge Z_{2n_j}(2\beta_j)^{L/(2n_j)}\). Their ratio bounds the thermal purity at length \(L\) by \(P_\tau(L,\beta_j)\ge[P_\tau(2n_j,\beta_j)P_{\mathrm s}(n_j,\beta_j)^2]^{L/(2n_j)}\ge(1-\varepsilon_j)^3\ge1-3\varepsilon_j\).

Since \(1-3\varepsilon_j\ge0.97>\tfrac12\), the purity bound \(w_0\ge P_\tau(L,\beta_j)>\tfrac12\) of Section 8 forces a single ground state to hold more than half the Boltzmann weight, proving ground-state uniqueness. The first excited weight satisfies \(w_1\le1-w_0\le3\varepsilon_j\), so \(e^{-\beta_j\Delta_L}=w_1/w_0\le3\varepsilon_j/(1-3\varepsilon_j)<20^{-2^j}\), and dividing by \(\beta_j=784\cdot2^j\) proves \(\Delta_L>\log20/784\).

The recurrence and the gap deduction are formalized in Lean 4: lean/CertifiedGaps/PurityCriterion.lean.

\[ e^{-\beta_j\Delta_L}=\frac{w_1}{w_0}\le\frac{3\varepsilon_j}{1-3\varepsilon_j}<20^{-2^j}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta_L>\frac{\log20}{784}. \]

17Certifying a seed: a toy example

To start the doubling iteration of Section 16, we still need the finite-scale seed bound of claim (I). Because the transfer operator \(X_\beta\) acts on the infinite-dimensional space of bond histories, we cannot find its eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) by diagonalizing \(X_\beta\) directly. Instead, a computer can evaluate exact traces \(Z_k=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,k}=\sum_i\lambda_i^k\) on small rings, which give power sums of the eigenvalues. Combining a small-ring power sum \(Z_6\) with a variational lower bound on \(Z_{24}\) at a larger length \(n=24\) suffices to certify the spatial purity \(P_{\mathrm s}(24)=Z_{48}/Z_{24}^2\) without solving for individual eigenvalues.

Consider a toy transfer operator whose true eigenvalues are \(1, 0.6, -0.5, 0.4\) (unknown to the certificate). First, on a \(6\)-site ring, the sixth moment is \(Z_6=\sum_i\lambda_i^6=1.0664\); since \(\lambda_i^6\ge0\), every eigenvalue satisfies \(|\lambda_i|\le1.0664^{1/6}=1.011\). Second, to rule out the possibility that \(Z_6\) is split across several comparable eigenvalues, we use a lower bound on \(Z_{24}\). Because \(Z_{24}=\sum_me^{-\beta E_m}\ge e^{-\beta E_0}\) and any trial state \(\psi\) gives a variational upper bound \(E_0\le\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle/\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\), exhibiting a trial state with negative energy (after shifting \(H\) by a constant per site, which cancels in all purities) proves \(Z_{24}\ge1\).

Now combine the upper bound \(Z_6=1.0664\) with the lower bound \(Z_{24}\ge1\). If every eigenvalue satisfied \(|\lambda_i|\le0.97\), each sixth power would satisfy \(\lambda_i^6\le0.97^6=0.833\). Because \(\lambda_i^{24}=(\lambda_i^6)^4\) and fourth powers are convex, a total budget of \(1.0664\) in sixth powers subject to \(\lambda_i^6\le0.833\) maximizes \(\sum_i\lambda_i^{24}\) when packed into one full weight of \(0.833\) and one remainder weight of \(1.0664-0.833=0.233\), giving \(Z_{24}\le0.833^4+0.233^4=0.484\), which contradicts \(Z_{24}\ge1\). Thus a single dominant eigenvalue \(\lambda_0\) must exceed \(0.97\) (\(\lambda_0^6>0.833\)). The remaining eigenvalues share at most \(0.233\) of the sixth-power sum, so their twenty-fourth powers sum to at most \(0.233^4=0.0030\). Keeping only \(\lambda_0^{48}\) in the numerator of \(P_{\mathrm s}(24)=\sum\lambda_i^{48}/(\sum\lambda_i^{24})^2\) and bounding the remainder of the denominator by \(0.233^4\) gives \(P_{\mathrm s}(24)\ge(1+0.233^4/0.97^{24})^{-2}=0.98765\ldots>0.987\). Section 18 applies the same three-step method to the spin-1 chain, using \(12\)-site rings, symmetry sectors, polynomial filters, and a \(72\)-site matrix-product trial state.

Toy numbers are exact to the digits shown; the spin-1 certificate (Section 18) uses degree-12 polynomial filters on symmetry sectors in models/spin1/.

\[ \sum_i\lambda_i^{24}\le\underbrace{0.833^4+(1.0664-0.833)^4}_{0.484}<1\le Z_{24}\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_0>0.97,\qquad P_{\mathrm s}(24)\ge\Big(1+\frac{0.233^4}{0.97^{24}}\Big)^{-2}=0.98765\ldots>0.987 . \]

18The spin-1 seed certificate

Claim (I) requires \(P_{\mathrm s}(2304,784)\ge0.99\) and \(P_\tau(4608,784)\ge0.99\). Because a \(2304\)-site ring has \(3^{2304}\) states, its partition function cannot be evaluated directly; instead, Ref. [2] certifies the spatial and thermal purities at the smaller seed scale \((n_0,\beta_0)=(36,49/4)\) and applies six doubling steps to reach \((2304,784)\). At \(\beta_0=49/4\), the certificate uses two finite inputs: exact partition functions of periodic and twisted rings of length \(n\le12\), and the exact energy of one matrix-product trial state on \(72\) sites. Just as in Section 17, each small ring gives one power sum of the transfer-operator eigenvalues, \(Z_n=\sum_i\lambda_i^n\) .

At the seed scale \((36,49/4)\), write the twelfth powers of the eigenvalues of \(X_{\beta_0}\) as nonnegative weights \(w_i=\lambda_i^{12}\), ordered with largest weight \(w_0\). Keeping only \(w_0^6\) in the numerator of the spatial purity gives

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(36)=\frac{\sum_iw_i^6}{(\sum_iw_i^3)^2}\ge\Big(1+\frac{R}{w_0^3}\Big)^{-2},\qquad R=\sum_{i\ne0}w_i^3 . \]

The purity is close to \(1\) when a single weight dominates: one dominant weight gives \(1\), whereas two equal weights give \(1/2\). Certifying \(P_{\mathrm s}(36)\) therefore reduces to two bounds on the ratio \(R/w_0^3\):

  • (a)The leading weight is large: \(w_0\ge0.988\).
  • (b)The subleading tail is small: \(R\le0.024\).

Together these two bounds give \(P_{\mathrm s}(36)\ge(1+0.024/0.988^3)^{-2}\ge0.952\). Because small rings give only power sums of weights rather than individual eigenvalues, we control the subleading weights by establishing an individual cap \(c\ge w_i\) for \(i\ne0\) , which bounds \(R\le c^2\sum_{i\ne0}w_i\) and also bounds the subleading terms of \(Z_{72}=\sum_iw_i^6\).

Part (a): lower-bounding the leading weight \(w_0\). First fix the energy normalization by shifting \(H\) by a constant \(\kappa\) per site so that the leading eigenvalue sits near \(1\); this shift multiplies \(Z_n\) by \(e^{-\beta\kappa n}\), which cancels in every purity. We then show that the leading eigenvalue exceeds \(\ell=0.999\), so \(w_0\ge0.999^{12}\simeq0.988\). If every eigenvalue satisfied \(|\lambda_i|\le\ell\), the leading term of \(Z_{72}=\sum_i\lambda_i^{72}\) would be at most \(\ell^{72}\simeq0.930\), and the caps developed in part (b) below limit all remaining terms of \(Z_{72}\) to less than \(0.001\), giving \(Z_{72}\le0.931\). Proving \(Z_{72}>1\) rules out this case and establishes \(w_0\ge0.988\).

To prove \(Z_{72}>1\) without diagonalizing the \(72\)-site ring, note that every Boltzmann weight is positive, so \(Z_{72}\ge e^{-\beta_0(E_0+72\kappa)}>1\) whenever the ground-state energy satisfies \(E_0<-72\kappa\). By the variational principle, any trial state \(\psi\) gives \(E_0\le\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle/\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\). Ref. [2] uses a translation-invariant matrix product state on \(72\) sites with exact integer entries and bond dimension \(26\). Contracting the \(72\) local transfer matrices in exact integer arithmetic gives a variational energy per site of \(-1.4014821126\ldots\); choosing \(\kappa=1.401482\) (rounded toward zero to six decimals) guarantees \(E_0<-72\kappa\) and hence \(Z_{72}>1\).

Part (b): polynomial filters and symmetry sectors. Each small ring provides one power sum over all eigenvalues. Already \(Z_{12}=\sum_iw_i\le1.90\) bounds every weight by \(1.90\). Sharper caps come from combining several rings into a nonnegative polynomial filter \(F(\lambda)=\sum_{n\le12}a_n\lambda^n\ge0\): since \(\sum_iF(\lambda_i)=\sum_na_nZ_n=:B\) is known from the small-ring traces, no eigenvalue can lie where \(F(\lambda)>B\). Choosing \(F(\lambda)=\lambda^4q(\lambda)^2\) with \(\deg q=4\) gives a nonnegative degree-\(12\) polynomial and caps every eigenvalue at \(|\lambda|\le1.0014\), or \(w_i\le1.02\).

A single sector-blind cap of \(1.02\) is not enough on its own: since \(Z_{12}\le1.90\) and \(w_0\ge0.988\), the remaining weights can still sum to \(0.91\), which under a cap of \(1.02\) could concentrate into a single second weight nearly as large as \(w_0\) , giving only \(P_{\mathrm s}(36)\ge0.31\). To separate \(w_0\) from the subleading weights, we split the transfer-operator spectrum into symmetry sectors so that \(w_0\) lies in a known sector and the remaining sectors can be filtered on their own.

These symmetry sectors come from global spin rotations, which leave every Heisenberg bond \(\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\) invariant. In the Cartesian basis of Section 6, the \(\pi\) rotation about the \(x\)-axis is \(P_x=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1)\), which leaves \(G_x\) invariant and negates \(G_y\) and \(G_z\). On a bond history \(a\), \(P_x\) therefore acts diagonally by the sign \(U_P(a)=(-1)^{\text{number of }y\text{ and }z\text{ events in }a}\). Inserting \(P_x^{-1}P_x=I\) into the site trace of Section 6 gives \(X_\beta(a,b)=U_P(a)\,X_\beta(a,b)\,U_P(b)\), so \(U_P\) commutes with \(X_\beta\). The same holds for the \(\pi\) rotations \(P_y\) and \(P_z\).

Because \(P_x,P_y,P_z\) commute with \(X_\beta\) and satisfy \(P_z=P_xP_y\), the transfer-operator spectrum splits into four \(\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2\) parity sectors: the symmetric sector \((+,+,+)\), which contains the leading weight \(w_0\), and three antisymmetric sectors \((+,-,-)\), \((-,+,-)\), \((-,-,+)\) (shown in grey ), which cyclic permutations \(x\to y\to z\) map into one another.

Because the three antisymmetric sectors have the same eigenvalues, we only need two sector power sums at each length \(n\): \(J_n=\sum_{i\in(+,+,+)}\lambda_i^n\) for the symmetric sector and \(N_n=\sum_{i\in(+,-,-)}\lambda_i^n\) for a single antisymmetric sector. The untwisted ring trace sums all four sectors, \(Z_n=J_n+3N_n\), while inserting \(U_P\) (for \(P=P_x\)) weights the four sectors by \((+1,+1,-1,-1)\), giving \(\operatorname{Tr}(X_\beta^nU_P)=J_n-N_n\). Solving these two equations yields \(N_n=\tfrac14(Z_n-\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^nU_P)\) and \(J_n=\tfrac14(Z_n+3\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^nU_P)\). Moving \(U_P\) onto a single physical bond turns \(\operatorname{Tr}(X_\beta^nU_P)\) into the partition function of an \(n\)-site ring with one twisted bond \(S_n^xS_1^x-S_n^yS_1^y-S_n^zS_1^z\), which Ref. [2] evaluates in exact integer arithmetic for all \(n\le12\).

These sector moments complete part (b) in four steps: (i) At \(n=12\), \(J_{12}\le1.066\) and \(N_{12}\le0.278\) (with \(1.066+3\times0.278=1.90=Z_{12}\)). (ii) Because every antisymmetric weight is bounded by \(N_{12}\le0.278\) while \(w_0\ge0.988\), the leading weight \(w_0\) lies in the symmetric sector. (iii) Applying the degree-\(12\) polynomial filter \(\Phi_N(\lambda)=\lambda^4(12-394\lambda^2+1000\lambda^4)^2\) to the antisymmetric moments \(N_4,\dots,N_{12}\) alone (where \(w_0\) is absent) gives \(\sum_{i\in(+,-,-)}\Phi_N(\lambda_i)=B_N\simeq4.8\times10^4\); since \(\Phi_N(\lambda)>B_N\) for \(|\lambda|\ge0.872\), every antisymmetric weight is capped at \(c=0.872^{12}\simeq0.19\). Leaving out \(w_0\) is what makes this cap sharp, as \(\Phi_N(0.999)\simeq3.8\times10^5\) would otherwise dominate the sum. (iv) Inside the symmetric sector, all subleading weights sum to at most \(J_{12}-w_0\le1.066-0.988\simeq0.08\).

Combining the three antisymmetric sectors and the subleading symmetric weights bounds the tail sum \(R=3\sum_{i\in(+,-,-)}w_i^3+\sum_{i\in(+,+,+),\,i\ne0}w_i^3\le3c^2N_{12}+0.08^3\simeq0.031\), and packing each antisymmetric sector up to the cap \(c\) sharpens this bound to \(R\le0.024\). Hence \(P_{\mathrm s}(36)\ge0.952\), or \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}\le0.0479\).

Thermal purity and six doubling steps. For \(P_\tau(72,49/4)=Z_{72}(49/2)/Z_{72}(49/4)^2\), the \(72\)-site trial state lower-bounds the numerator by \(1\) and the sector caps upper-bound the denominator by \(1.22\), giving \(P_\tau(72,49/4)\ge0.8185\) (\(\varepsilon_\tau\le0.1815\)). Although \(\varepsilon_\tau=0.1815\) exceeds the symmetric threshold \(\varepsilon_*\approx0.12731\), the small spatial defect \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm s}\le0.0479\) pulls the thermal defect down under the asymmetric doubling recurrence: six doubling steps carry \((36,49/4)\) to \((2304,784)\) with defects \((0.0084513,0.0054986)\), both below \(0.01\), proving claim (I).

Temperature lifting to \(L\ge36\). Because the seed at \((36,49/4)\) needed six doubling steps before its thermal defect dropped below \(0.01\), the bound above begins only at \(L=2304\). To reach smaller lengths and a sharper gap bound without recomputing the small-ring traces, note that positive Boltzmann weights \(x_m=e^{-\beta E_m}>0\) satisfy \(\sum_m x_m^r\le(\sum_m x_m)^r\) for any \(r\ge1\), which gives \(Z_k(r\beta)\le Z_k(\beta)^r\). Applying this inequality with \(r=17/8\) lifts the small-ring trace bounds from Ref. [2] at \(\beta_0=49/4\) directly to \(r\beta_0=833/32\), where excited weights are much smaller. Combined with a \(120\)-site trial state, this lifted seed certifies \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm s},\varepsilon_\tau)=(0.031,0.048)\) at \((n_0,\beta_0)=(60,833/32)\) and proves \(\Delta_L>0.0729\) for every even \(L\ge36\), about \(19\) times \(\log20/784\).

Every number in the spin-1 seed is an integer or an exact fraction: models/spin1/results/scalars.json.

Why integer entries? Any trial state, for example one from DMRG, would do if its energy is evaluated rigorously. The trial energy lies below −κ by only 1.1 × 10−7 per site, the remainder from rounding κ, so a floating-point error could flip the sign; integers make the comparison exact.

The other models in this repository have shorter correlation lengths: they diagonalize rings of up to 16 spins completely and bound every floating-point rounding error.

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(36)\ge\Big(1+\frac{R}{w_0^3}\Big)^{-2},\qquad w_0\ge0.999^{12},\qquad R\le3c^2N_{12}+0.08^3,\qquad c=0.872^{12}, \] \[ N_n=\tfrac14\big(Z_n-\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^nU_P\big),\qquad (0.0479,\ 0.1815)\ \xrightarrow{\ \times6\ }\ (0.0084513,\ 0.0054986). \]

Part II · Open chains

Cutting the ring open into a chain with two free ends and pinning the two endpoint spins with a boundary field \(h=3/5\) replaces the periodic trace \(\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}\) by an inner product \(\langle v_\beta|X_\beta^{\,n}|v_\beta\rangle\) between boundary vectors. Tracking the overlap of \(v_\beta\) with the leading eigenvector of \(X_\beta\) across the doubling scales proves that every odd open chain of length \(n\ge1921\) has a unique ground state and a uniform spectral gap [3]; see Part II: Open chains.

\(G_\alpha\)\(iS^\alpha\) for \(\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}\): real antisymmetric \(3\times3\) spin-1 generators in the Cartesian basis \(|x\rangle,|y\rangle,|z\rangle\), satisfying \(-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}=\sum_\alpha G_{\alpha,j}\otimes G_{\alpha,j+1}\).6
\(a,\ b\)Bond histories: time-ordered lists of bond events \((\tau,\alpha)\) on a site’s left and right bonds.6
\(\mu_\beta\)Poisson measure over the histories of a single bond: weight \(d\tau\) per event, total measure \(\sum_k(3\beta)^k/k!=e^{3\beta}\).6
\(W_\beta^{ss'}(a,b)\)Time-ordered product of event matrices \(G_\alpha\) along one site’s world line: the local tensor of the thermal MPO.6
\(X_\beta(a,b)\)Spatial transfer operator \(\sum_s W_\beta^{ss}(a,b)\), satisfying \(Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}\).6
\(\lambda_i(\beta)\)Eigenvalues of \(X_\beta\) (with leading eigenvalue \(\lambda_0\)), so \(Z_L(\beta)=\sum_i\lambda_i(\beta)^L\).7
\(\sigma_n,\ \rho_n\)Normalized spatial state \(X_\beta^{\,n}/Z_n(\beta)\) and thermal Gibbs state \(e^{-\beta H_n}/Z_n(\beta)\).9
\(P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta),\ P_\tau(n,\beta)\)Spatial purity \(Z_{2n}(\beta)/Z_n(\beta)^2=\operatorname{Tr}\sigma_n^2\) and thermal purity \(Z_n(2\beta)/Z_n(\beta)^2=\operatorname{Tr}\rho_n^2\).9
\(f(z)\)Squaring map \(z^2/[2(1-z)^2]\), which upper-bounds the purity defect after squaring a state of defect \(z\).10
Reflection positivitySymmetric bond decomposition \(-h=\sum_\alpha C_\alpha\otimes\overline{C_\alpha}\) (with real symmetric on-site term \(o\)), which ensures that \(X_\beta\) is self-adjoint.13
\(\varepsilon_*\)Symmetric admissibility threshold \(\approx0.12731\): whenever \(\max(\varepsilon_{\mathrm s},\varepsilon_\tau)<\varepsilon_*\), the doubling recurrence contracts both defects to zero.15
\((n_0,\beta_0)\)Seed ring length and inverse temperature (\((36,49/4)\) for the periodic spin-1 certificate).18
\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm s},\ \varepsilon_\tau\)Certified upper bounds on the seed spatial defect \(1-P_{\mathrm s}(n_0,\beta_0)\) and thermal defect \(1-P_\tau(2n_0,\beta_0)\).18
