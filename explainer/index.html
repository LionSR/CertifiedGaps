The spacetime bootstrap

A proof that the spin-1 Heisenberg ring has a spectral gap, in nineteen figures, with an open-chain sequel

A quantum spin chain has discrete energy levels, and its spectral gap \(\Delta\) is the energy step from the lowest level (the ground state) to the first excited level. We want to prove that as a ring of spin-1 sites grows to any even length \(L\), this gap \(\Delta_L\) never shrinks below a fixed positive constant.

At low temperature (large inverse temperature \(\beta\)), higher-energy states are suppressed by Boltzmann factors \(e^{-\beta E}\), so excited states fade relative to the ground state like \(e^{-\beta\Delta}\). A gap is therefore a decay rate in \(\beta\). The proof measures how close the thermal state is to a single pure state—its purity—on one finite ring, then shows that doubling the ring length and the inverse temperature together improves the purity automatically, step after step, all the way to infinity. Each section below states the question it solves, walks through the step in an animated figure, and ends with the formula.

1What is a gap?

Consider a periodic ring of \(L\) quantum spins (where site \(L+1\) is identified with site \(1\)), interacting with their nearest neighbors through the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg Hamiltonian \(H_L=\sum_{j=1}^L\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\). Here \(\mathbf S_j=(S_j^x,S_j^y,S_j^z)\) are the three spin-1 operators on site \(j\). Because the ring is finite, \(H_L\) has discrete energy levels \(E_0(L)\le E_1(L)\le\cdots\). The lowest energy \(E_0(L)\) is the ground-state energy, and the spectral gap \(\Delta_L=E_1(L)-E_0(L)\) is the energy step from the ground level to the first excited level.

On any single finite ring, \(\Delta_L\) is just a difference of matrix eigenvalues; the hard question is whether \(\Delta_L\) stays open as \(L\to\infty\). Haldane predicted in 1983 that while the spin-\(\tfrac12\) chain is gapless (\(\Delta_L\to0\)), the spin-1 chain keeps a positive gap as the ring grows; numerical calculations give \(\Delta\approx0.41\). For decades there was no proof that a single fixed constant \(c>0\) bounds \(\Delta_L\ge c\) for every large even ring length \(L\).

Haldane, 1983 [1]. The theorem here is from OpenAI’s 2026 preprint [2]; this page follows the main note [4].

\[ H_L=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1},\qquad \Delta_L=E_1(L)-E_0(L). \]

Theorem: \(\Delta_L>\log 20/784\approx0.0038\) for every even \(L\ge2304\), and the ground state is unique.

2A gap is a decay rate

Finding \(E_0(L)\) and \(E_1(L)\) directly by diagonalizing \(H_L\) is impossible on long rings, because \(H_L\) is a \(3^L\times 3^L\) matrix. Thermal equilibrium turns the energy gap into a rate of probability decay that we can bound without solving for individual energy levels.

At inverse temperature \(\beta\) (where large \(\beta\) means low temperature), energy level \(m\) carries the Boltzmann weight \(e^{-\beta E_m}\) and has probability \(w_m=e^{-\beta E_m}/Z_L(\beta)\), where \(Z_L(\beta)=\sum_m e^{-\beta E_m}=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H_L}\) is the partition function of the \(L\)-site ring. In the ratio of the first two probabilities, the normalizing denominator \(Z_L(\beta)\) cancels, leaving \(w_1/w_0=e^{-\beta(E_1-E_0)}=e^{-\beta\Delta_L}\).

On a logarithmic plot against \(\beta\), \(\log(w_1/w_0)=-\beta\Delta_L\) is a straight line of slope \(-\Delta_L\). To prove \(\Delta_L\ge c\), it is therefore enough to show that the excited weight ratio \(w_1/w_0\) decays at least as fast as \(e^{-c\beta}\), with the same constant \(c>0\) for every ring length \(L\). Everything that follows is built to control excited thermal weight at low temperature on arbitrarily long rings.

\[ \frac{w_1}{w_0}=e^{-\beta\Delta_L}\le e^{-c\beta}\ \text{ for all }\beta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta_L\ge c. \]

3Imaginary time

Why does the thermal operator \(e^{-\beta H_L}\) isolate the ground state, and why is \(\beta\) called imaginary time? Expand any quantum state in the energy eigenstates \(|m\rangle\) of \(H_L\) as \(\psi=\sum_m c_m|m\rangle\), where each complex coefficient \(c_m\) has magnitude \(|c_m|\) and probability \(|c_m|^2\).

In ordinary real time \(t\), quantum evolution \(e^{-iHt}\psi\) multiplies each coefficient \(c_m\) by the phase factor \(e^{-iE_mt}\). Viewed as an arrow in the complex plane, each amplitude \(c_m\) simply rotates at speed \(E_m\); its length never changes, so every probability \(|c_m|^2\) stays the same forever and nothing settles.

Now replace real time \(t\) by imaginary time \(t=-i\tau\) with \(\tau>0\). The oscillating phase \(e^{-iE_mt}\) becomes a real damping factor \(e^{-\tau E_m}\). Nothing rotates anymore; instead, each component shrinks without turning, and higher energies shrink fastest. After rescaling the state so that the probabilities again sum to \(1\), the ground state wins and the first excited amplitude ratio \(|c_1(\tau)|/|c_0(\tau)|\) decays at rate \(e^{-\tau\Delta}\). At \(\tau=\beta\), these damping factors \(e^{-\beta E_m}\) are the exact Boltzmann weights of Section 2: "long imaginary time" and "low temperature" are the same mathematical statement.

\[ e^{-iHt}\psi=\sum_m c_me^{-iE_mt}|m\rangle\ \xrightarrow{\ t=-i\tau\ }\ e^{-\tau H}\psi=\sum_m c_me^{-\tau E_m}|m\rangle,\qquad \frac{|c_1(\tau)|}{|c_0(\tau)|}\propto e^{-\tau\Delta}. \]

4A sum over histories

To compute the partition function \(\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}\) without diagonalizing \(H\), we factor the imaginary-time interval \(\beta\) into \(M\) tiny steps of size \(d\tau=\beta/M\), writing \(e^{-\beta H}=A^M\) with single-step matrix \(A=e^{-d\tau H}\approx I-d\tau H\). One algebraic fact then does all the work: the definition of matrix multiplication turns a matrix power \(A^M\) into a sum over step-by-step histories.

For two matrices \(A\) and \(B\), the entry in row \(i\) and column \(k\) of their product is \((AB)_{ik}=\sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}\): to go from state \(i\) to state \(k\) in two steps, pass through an intermediate state \(j\), multiply the two step entries \(A_{ij}\) and \(B_{jk}\) used along that route, and add over every possible \(j\). For example, with \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\), the top-left entry of \(A^2\) sums two routes, \(1\to1\to1\) (weight \(2\times2=4\)) and \(1\to2\to1\) (weight \(1\times1=1\)), giving \((A^2)_{11}=4+1=5\). Repeating this rule across \(M\) factors, an entry \((A^M)_{ij}\) is a sum over all \(M\)-step paths \(i=s_0\to s_1\to\cdots\to s_M=j\), where each path is weighted by the product of the matrix entries it uses.

Apply this to a single spin with states \(|\!\uparrow\rangle,|\!\downarrow\rangle\) and Hamiltonian \(H=-\sigma^x=-\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\). For a tiny step \(d\tau\), the step matrix is \(A=e^{-d\tau H}\approx\begin{pmatrix}1&d\tau\\d\tau&1\end{pmatrix}\): staying in the same spin state has weight \(1\) (diagonal entries), while flipping to the opposite state has weight \(d\tau\) (off-diagonal entries). As \(d\tau\to0\), a path becomes a continuous-time world line on \([0,\beta]\) that stays put except at \(k\) flip times. Each flip carries weight \(d\tau\), and choosing \(k\) ordered flip times in \([0,\beta]\) gives volume \(\beta^k/k!\). The trace \(\operatorname{Tr}(A^M)=\sum_i(A^M)_{ii}\) keeps only periodic paths that end in the state where they started, so \(k\) must be even and there are \(2\) starting states (\(\uparrow\) or \(\downarrow\)). Summing \(2\beta^k/k!\) over even \(k\) gives \(2\cosh\beta\), the exact value of \(\operatorname{Tr}e^{\beta\sigma^x}\). This sum over continuous-time trajectories is the Euclidean, or imaginary-time, path integral.

The same identity counts walks on a graph: (Aᴹ)ᵢⱼ with A the adjacency matrix.

\[ (A^M)_{ij}=\sum_{\text{paths }i\to j}\ \prod_{\text{steps}}A_{s_ts_{t+1}},\qquad \operatorname{Tr}e^{\beta\sigma^x}=\sum_{k\ \mathrm{even}}\frac{2\beta^k}{k!}=2\cosh\beta . \]

5The partition function is a torus

Now pass from one spin to a ring of \(L\) spins. Lay the \(L\) sites horizontally along space and plot imaginary time \(\tau\in[0,\beta]\) vertically, forming an \(L\times\beta\) spacetime sheet.

Both pairs of opposite edges of this sheet are glued together into circles. Vertically, splitting \(e^{-\beta H_L}=(e^{-\beta H_L/M})^M\) into \(M\) imaginary-time steps and taking the trace glues the final time slice \(\tau=\beta\) back to the initial slice \(\tau=0\), turning imaginary time into a circle of circumference \(\beta\). Horizontally, the periodic ring boundary condition glues site \(L\) back to site \(1\), turning space into a circle of circumference \(L\).

With both space and imaginary time periodic, the partition function \(Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H_L}\) is a sum over histories on an \(L\times\beta\) spacetime torus. The proof uses the exact continuous-time limit (\(M\to\infty\)) of this torus, so there is no time-discretization error.

\[ Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H_L}=\operatorname{Tr}\big(e^{-\beta H_L/M}\big)^M . \]

6Histories of a chain

To see how \(Z_L(\beta)\) depends on the ring length \(L\), we want to contract the spacetime torus one site at a time around the ring. A classical warm-up shows the blueprint: on a classical Ising ring of \(L\) spins \(s_j=\pm1\), each neighboring pair \((s_j,s_{j+1})\) contributes a bond weight \(T(s_j,s_{j+1})=e^{Ks_js_{j+1}}\) (\(e^K\) when neighbors agree, \(e^{-K}\) when they disagree). Draw the \(2\times2\) transfer matrix \(T\) as a box with two horizontal legs for its row and column spin indices. Joining the leg \(s_2\) shared by the first two boxes means summing over \(s_2=\pm1\), which is matrix multiplication \(\sum_{s_2}T(s_1,s_2)T(s_2,s_3)=(T^2)(s_1,s_3)\). Joining all \(L\) boxes around the ring and closing the last leg back to the first traces the matrix product: \(Z=\operatorname{Tr}T^L\).

The quantum spin-1 chain works the exact same way once we replace the classical spin on each leg by a bond history. In the Cartesian spin-1 basis \(|x\rangle,|y\rangle,|z\rangle\), define the three operators \(G_\alpha=iS^\alpha\) for \(\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}\). These are real antisymmetric \(3\times3\) matrices: for example, \(G_z=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\) rotates \(|x\rangle\to-|y\rangle\) and \(|y\rangle\to|x\rangle\) while killing \(|z\rangle\), so \(G_zG_z=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,0)\) has trace \(-2\). Because \((S^\alpha)^2=-G_\alpha^2\), each bond term in \(-H_L\) decomposes as \(-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}=\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}G_{\alpha,j}\otimes G_{\alpha,j+1}\).

When we expand \(e^{-\beta H_L}\approx(I-d\tau H_L)^M\) in tiny steps \(d\tau\), each step either does nothing (weight \(1\)) or fires one bond term \(G_{\alpha,j}\otimes G_{\alpha,j+1}\) (weight \(d\tau\)). Each such action is a bond event: a rung across bond \((j,j+1)\) at an imaginary time \(\tau\in(0,\beta)\), labeled by \(\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}\), which places the real \(3\times3\) matrix \(G_\alpha\) on both site \(j\) and site \(j+1\) at time \(\tau\). The list of all events on a single bond during \(0<\tau<\beta\) is called a bond history, and this bond history is the object passed from site to site along the ring.

Once the bond histories are fixed, different sites commute, so the trace over the chain factors into one trace per site. Follow a single site's world line from spin state \(|s'\rangle\) at \(\tau=0\) to \(\langle s|\) at \(\tau=\beta\): events from its left bond history \(a\) and its right bond history \(b\) multiply in chronological order (the time-ordering symbol \(\mathcal T_\tau\) places later imaginary times to the left) to form the local four-leg tensor \(W_\beta^{ss'}(a,b)\). Gluing \(\tau=\beta\) back to \(\tau=0\) sets \(s=s'\) and sums over \(s\in\{x,y,z\}\), tracing that matrix product into the transfer kernel \(X_\beta(a,b)\): for example, no events gives \(\operatorname{Tr}I=3\); one \(z\)-event on each side gives \(\operatorname{Tr}G_zG_z=-2\); and \(a=\{z\text{ at }0.2,\,x\text{ at }0.6\}\) with \(b=\{y\text{ at }0.4\}\) gives \(\operatorname{Tr}(G_xG_yG_z)=1\). Joining the shared bond-history legs around all \(L\) sites sums and integrates over every bond history, giving \(Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}\).

Gα = iSα in the Cartesian basis |x⟩, |y⟩, |z⟩ are real antisymmetric 3 × 3 matrices.

7Two ways to slice the torus

We now have two ways to slice the exact same \(L\times\beta\) spacetime torus, and equating them connects the energy spectrum of the \(L\)-site chain to a single operator that does not depend on \(L\).

Slicing the torus horizontally across space at fixed moments of imaginary time propagates the \(L\)-spin state upward by \(e^{-\beta H_L}\), giving the familiar sum over energy eigenvalues \(Z_L(\beta)=\sum_m e^{-\beta E_m(L)}\). Slicing vertically along imaginary time instead isolates one site's full time history on \([0,\beta]\) and carries it from site to site around the ring by the transfer operator \(X_\beta\). Closing the ring of \(L\) sites traces \(X_\beta^{\,L}\), giving \(Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}=\sum_i\lambda_i(\beta)^L\).

In the first sum, the energies \(E_m(L)\) change with \(L\); in the second, the eigenvalues \(\lambda_i(\beta)\) of \(X_\beta\) depend on \(\beta\) alone, and the ring length \(L\) enters only as an exponent. Just as low temperature (large \(\beta\)) makes the lowest energy \(E_0\) dominate the horizontal sum, a long ring (large \(L\)) makes the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_0(\beta)\) dominate the vertical sum.

\[ Z_L(\beta)=\sum_m e^{-\beta E_m}=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}=\sum_i\lambda_i(\beta)^L . \]

8Purity, seen as squares

In Section 2 we saw that to bound the gap, we need the ground state to carry almost all of the Boltzmann probability \(w_0\), leaving very little for \(w_1\). How can we prove that a single probability \(p_0\) dominates a distribution without computing the individual probabilities \(p_i\)?

For any probability list \(p_0\ge p_1\ge\cdots\ge0\) adding to \(1\) (or any positive density matrix with unit trace and eigenvalues \(p_i\)), the purity is the sum of squared probabilities \(P=\sum_i p_i^2\), and \(1-P\) is the purity defect. Lay the probabilities end to end along a segment of length \(\sum_i p_i=1\) and draw a square of side \(p_i\) on each piece: the total area of the squares is the purity \(P=\sum_i p_i^2\).

Because every square is no taller than the largest one \(p_0\), all the squares fit inside a rectangle of width \(1\) and height \(p_0\). Thus \(P\le p_0\): the purity is a direct lower bound on the largest probability. A purity of \(0.99\) (defect \(0.01\)) guarantees that a single dominant state holds at least \(99\%\) of the probability, and all other states together hold at most \(1\%\).

\[ P=\sum_ip_i^2\ \le\ p_0\sum_ip_i=p_0 . \]

9Two purities from one torus

Why is purity so useful on the spacetime torus? Because cutting the closed torus network open in either direction produces a normalized state, and computing the purity of that state simply glues two tori together into a single torus twice as large.

Write \(Z_n(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,n}\) for the partition function of an \(n\)-site ring at inverse temperature \(\beta\), drawn as a closed loop of \(n\) transfer boxes \(X_\beta\). First, cut the torus vertically along imaginary time between two sites, severing one bond-history leg of the ring. What remains is the chain of \(n\) boxes \(X_\beta^{\,n}\) with two open legs. Dividing by \(Z_n(\beta)\) so that its trace is \(1\) gives the spatial state \(\sigma_n=X_\beta^{\,n}/Z_n(\beta)\), with eigenvalues \(p_i=\lambda_i(\beta)^n/Z_n(\beta)\). Its purity \(\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_n^2)\) joins two copies of \(\sigma_n\) leg to leg into a closed ring of \(2n\) boxes: the spatial purity \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}(X_\beta^{\,2n})/Z_n(\beta)^2=Z_{2n}(\beta)/Z_n(\beta)^2\), which glues two tori side by side.

Second, cut the torus horizontally across all \(n\) sites at one moment of imaginary time \(\tau\). Opening the time circle leaves the operator \(e^{-\beta H_n}\) with open spin legs at \(\tau=0\) and \(\tau=\beta\). Dividing by \(Z_n(\beta)\) gives the thermal Gibbs state \(\rho_n=e^{-\beta H_n}/Z_n(\beta)\), with eigenvalues \(w_m=e^{-\beta E_m(n)}/Z_n(\beta)\). Its purity \(\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_n^2)=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-2\beta H_n})/Z_n(\beta)^2\) stacks two copies vertically in imaginary time to form a torus of height \(2\beta\): the thermal purity \(P_\tau(n,\beta)=Z_n(2\beta)/Z_n(\beta)^2\). Both purities are ratios of partition functions of equal total spacetime area \(2n\beta\), so adding any constant shift to \(H_n\) multiplies numerator and denominator by the same factor and changes neither purity.

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)=\frac{Z_{2n}(\beta)}{Z_n(\beta)^2},\qquad P_\tau(n,\beta)=\frac{Z_n(2\beta)}{Z_n(\beta)^2}. \]

10Squaring sharpens

Once a state has purity \(P=1-\varepsilon\) close to \(1\), how do we make it much purer? By gluing two copies of the torus together, which squares the state and renormalizes its probabilities.

In space, gluing two copies of \(\sigma_n=X_\beta^{\,n}/Z_n(\beta)\) gives \(\sigma_n^2=X_\beta^{\,2n}/Z_n(\beta)^2=P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)\,\sigma_{2n}\), so \(\sigma_n^2/\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_n^2)=\sigma_{2n}\) is the spatial state of the ring twice as long. In imaginary time, stacking two copies of \(\rho_n(\beta)\) gives \(\rho_n(\beta)^2=P_\tau(n,\beta)\,\rho_n(2\beta)\), the Gibbs state at twice the inverse temperature. In both cases, each probability \(p_i\) is replaced by \(p_i'=p_i^2/P\), which suppresses small probabilities relative to the leader \(p_0\).

11Space and time are tied

Squaring \(\rho_n(\beta)\) improves the thermal purity by doubling \(\beta\), but it never increases the ring length \(n\). Conversely, at fixed \(\beta\), making the ring longer gives thermal excitations more room and lowers the purity. To keep the purity high on arbitrarily long rings, \(n\) and \(\beta\) must double together, which requires an identity that trades spatial purity for thermal purity.

First notice what a single gluing does to the partition function. Rearranging the two definitions from Section 9 gives \(Z_{2n}(\beta)=P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)\,Z_n(\beta)^2\) for a side-by-side spatial gluing, and \(Z_n(2\beta)=P_\tau(n,\beta)\,Z_n(\beta)^2\) for a vertical thermal stacking: each gluing of two identical tori multiplies the squared partition function by the corresponding purity.

Now start with four identical \(n\times\beta\) tori, with product \(Z_n(\beta)^4\), and glue them into a single \(2n\times2\beta\) torus \(Z_{2n}(2\beta)\) in two different orders. Gluing rows first (side by side in space) multiplies by \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)^2\) to make two \(2n\times\beta\) tori \(Z_{2n}(\beta)^2\), and stacking those two vertically multiplies by \(P_\tau(2n,\beta)\). Gluing columns first (stacking in imaginary time) multiplies by \(P_\tau(n,\beta)^2\) to make two \(n\times2\beta\) tori \(Z_n(2\beta)^2\), and gluing those two side by side multiplies by \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,2\beta)\). Both routes produce the exact same torus \(Z_{2n}(2\beta)\), so their purity products are equal. Because every purity is at most \(1\), dividing by \(P_\tau(n,\beta)^2\le1\) turns a spatial bound at \(\beta\) and a thermal bound at \(2n\) into a spatial bound at \(2\beta\).

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n,2\beta)\,P_\tau(n,\beta)^2=\frac{Z_{2n}(2\beta)}{Z_n(\beta)^4}=P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)^2\,P_\tau(2n,\beta). \]

12Why the proof needs a symmetric transfer matrix

Before combining squaring and coupling, we must check a hidden assumption. In Sections 8–11 we treated the eigenvalues \(p_i=\lambda_i^n/Z_n\) of the spatial state \(\sigma_n=X_\beta^{\,n}/Z_n\) as nonnegative probabilities summing to \(1\). Why are the eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) of \(X_\beta\) real, so that \(\lambda_i^n\ge0\) for every even ring length \(n\)?

A \(2\times2\) numerical test makes the danger concrete. For the symmetric matrix \(X=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.3\\0.3&0.5\end{pmatrix}\), the eigenvalues \(1.06\) and \(0.34\) are real, so for \(n=1,2,3,4,\dots\) the spatial purity \(Z_{2n}/Z_n^2=\operatorname{Tr}(X^{2n})/(\operatorname{Tr}X^n)^2\) runs \(0.63, 0.83, 0.94, 0.98, \dots\), staying in \((0,1]\) and climbing smoothly to \(1\). For the slightly rotated non-symmetric matrix \(X=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&-0.4\\0.4&0.9\end{pmatrix}\), whose eigenvalues \(0.9\pm0.4i\) are complex, \(\operatorname{Tr}X^n\) changes sign and the same ratio \(Z_{2n}/Z_n^2\) takes the values \(0.40, -0.11, -4.2, -47\): no probability distribution can do that, and both the squaring inequality and \(P_{\mathrm s}\le1\) would collapse. The proof therefore requires \(X_\beta\) to be a real symmetric operator.

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n)=\frac{Z_{2n}}{Z_n^2}=\sum_ip_i^2\in(0,1]\quad\text{only if}\quad p_i=\frac{\lambda_i^n}{Z_n}\ \text{are real and}\ \ge0 . \]

13Where the symmetry comes from

What makes the transfer matrix \(X_\beta\) symmetric? View \(X_\beta(a,b)\) as a matrix whose row index is the left bond history \(a\) and whose column index is the right bond history \(b\). Symmetry \(X_\beta(a,b)=X_\beta(b,a)\) means that mirroring a site's transfer box horizontally—swapping the histories arriving from its left and right bonds—leaves the trace unchanged.

This left–right symmetry comes directly from the bond Hamiltonian. In \(X_\beta(a,b)\), a site collects all events arriving from its left bond (\(a\)) and from its right bond (\(b\)) and multiplies their \(3\times3\) matrices in time order. For the Heisenberg antiferromagnet, \(-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}=\sum_\alpha G_\alpha\otimes G_\alpha\), so every event of type \(\alpha\) places the exact same real matrix \(G_\alpha\) on both sites of its bond. A site therefore receives the same matrix \(G_\alpha\) whether the event arrives from its left bond or its right bond, and cannot tell the two sides apart: with \(a=\{G_z\ \text{at}\ 0.2,\ G_x\ \text{at}\ 0.6\}\) and \(b=\{G_y\ \text{at}\ 0.4\}\), both \(X(a,b)\) and \(X(b,a)\) multiply the same three matrices at the same times and equal \(\operatorname{Tr}G_xG_yG_z=1\).

A bond that inserts different matrices on its two ends breaks this mirror symmetry, because a site is the right end of its left bond and the left end of its right bond: with \(G_z\) on the left site of a bond and \(P=\mathrm{diag}(1,0,0)\) on the right site, two events give \(\operatorname{Tr}(P^2)=1\) from the left bond and \(\operatorname{Tr}(G_z^2)=-2\) from the right bond (or, in the figure's positive-diagonal variant with \(Q=-G_z^2=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,0)\) on the left site and \(P\) on the right, \(\operatorname{Tr}(P^2)=1\) from the left and \(\operatorname{Tr}(Q^2)=2\) from the right). Reflection positivity is the general condition that the bond looks the same from both sides, \(-h=\sum_kC_k\otimes\Theta(C_k)\), with positive weights.

14Testing a bond for reflection positivity

Given any two-site bond term \(h\), how can we check whether \(-h\) has a reflection-positive decomposition without guessing the matrices \(C_k\) by hand? On two spin-1 sites, \(-h\) is a four-leg tensor with \(3^4=81\) entries \(\langle ij|{-h}|kl\rangle\), where \(i,k\in\{x,y,z\}\) are the output and input indices on the left site and \(j,l\in\{x,y,z\}\) are the output and input indices on the right site. In the usual \(9\times9\) matrix of \(-h\), rows are the two outputs \((i,j)\) and columns are the two inputs \((k,l)\).

To test the bond, regroup those same \(81\) entries into a new \(9\times9\) matrix \(R[-h]\) whose rows carry both indices \((i,k)\) of the left site and whose columns carry both indices \((j,l)\) of the right site: \(R[-h]_{(ik),(jl)}=\langle ij|{-h}|kl\rangle\). This regrouping of indices is called bond realignment. Writing \(\mathrm{vec}(A)\) for the \(9\)-component column vector of entries \(A_{ik}\) of a \(3\times3\) matrix \(A\), realignment turns a tensor product \(A\otimes B\) (with entries \(A_{ik}B_{jl}\)) into the rank-one outer product \(\mathrm{vec}(A)\,\mathrm{vec}(B)^{\mathsf T}\).

Under realignment, a reflection-positive bond \(-h=\sum_\alpha C_\alpha\otimes\overline{C_\alpha}\) becomes a sum of squares \(\sum_\alpha v_\alpha v_\alpha^\dagger\) with \(v_\alpha=\mathrm{vec}(C_\alpha)\)—a positive semidefinite matrix (\(\succeq0\)) whose eigenvalues are all \(\ge0\). For the spin-1 Heisenberg antiferromagnet (\(h=\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\)), \(R[-h]=\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)\,\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)^{\mathsf T}\) has eigenvalues \(2, 2, 2\) and six zeros, so it passes; the ferromagnet (\(h=-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\)) gives \(-2, -2, -2\) and fails, and no on-site constant shift can repair all three negative eigenvalues.

Rule for bilinear chains: reflection positive exactly when an even number of couplings Jx, Jy, Jz are ferromagnetic.

\[ R\big[A\otimes B\big]=\mathrm{vec}(A)\,\mathrm{vec}(B)^{\mathsf T},\qquad R\big[-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\big]=\sum_\alpha\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)\,\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)^{\mathsf T}\succeq0 . \]

15One doubling step

We can now assemble the self-improving loop at the heart of the spacetime bootstrap. Suppose at an even ring length \(n\) and inverse temperature \(\beta\) we know that both the spatial purity \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)\) and the doubled-length thermal purity \(P_\tau(2n,\beta)\) are at least \(1-\varepsilon\), with defect \(\varepsilon\le1/100\). Four alternating moves—coupling, squaring in space, coupling, and squaring in time—carry these two bounds to the doubled scale \((2n,2\beta)\):

  1. Couple to double \(\beta\) in space: The coupling identity of Section 11 gives \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,2\beta)=P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)^2P_\tau(2n,\beta)/P_\tau(n,\beta)^2\). Since \(P_\tau(n,\beta)\le1\), dropping the denominator leaves \(P_{\mathrm s}(n,2\beta)\ge(1-\varepsilon)^3\ge1-3\varepsilon\), costing a factor of at most \(3\) in the defect.
  2. Square in space to double \(n\): Because \(X_{2\beta}\) is symmetric and \(n\) is even, squaring \(\sigma_n(2\beta)\) to \(\sigma_{2n}(2\beta)\) applies the squaring function \(f\) of Section 10, turning the defect \(1-(1-\varepsilon)^3\le3\varepsilon\) into \(\delta=f(1-(1-\varepsilon)^3)\approx\tfrac92\varepsilon^2\le5\varepsilon^2\) (for \(\varepsilon\le1/100\)). Thus \(P_{\mathrm s}(2n,2\beta)\ge1-5\varepsilon^2\).
  3. Couple at length \(2n\): Applying the coupling identity at size \(2n\) gives \(P_\tau(4n,\beta)=P_\tau(2n,\beta)^2P_{\mathrm s}(2n,2\beta)/P_{\mathrm s}(2n,\beta)^2\ge(1-\varepsilon)^2(1-\delta)\ge(1-\varepsilon)^3\), a defect of about \(2\varepsilon\) (and at most \(3\varepsilon\)).
  4. Square in time to double \(\beta\) at length \(4n\): Squaring the Gibbs state \(\rho_{4n}(\beta)\) to \(\rho_{4n}(2\beta)\) applies \(f\) once more, giving \(P_\tau(4n,2\beta)\ge1-\delta\ge1-5\varepsilon^2\).

The output pair at \((2n,2\beta)\) has the exact same form as the input pair at \((n,\beta)\), with the defect \(\varepsilon\) replaced by \(5\varepsilon^2\) (and the exact map \(\varepsilon\mapsto\delta\) keeps improving whenever \(\varepsilon<\varepsilon_*\approx0.127\)).

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta),\,P_\tau(2n,\beta)\ge1-\varepsilon\ \Longrightarrow\ P_{\mathrm s}(2n,2\beta),\,P_\tau(4n,2\beta)\ge1-5\varepsilon^2 . \]

16Repeat, and read off the gap

Once the starting defect is small enough that \(5\varepsilon_0<1\), repeating the doubling step drives the defect down doubly exponentially in the step number \(j\)—which is ordinary exponential decay in the inverse temperature \(\beta_j\). Starting from \(\varepsilon_0=1/100\) at \((n_0,\beta_0)=(2304,784)\), \(j\) steps of \(\varepsilon_{j+1}=5\varepsilon_j^2\) give \(\varepsilon_j=20^{-2^j}/5\) at \((n_j,\beta_j)=(2304\cdot2^j,\,784\cdot2^j)\). Because \(2^j=\beta_j/784\), this is \(\varepsilon_j=\tfrac15e^{-\beta_j\log20/784}\): on a log plot against \(\beta\), the points fall on a straight line of slope \(-\log20/784\).

To cover every even ring length \(L\ge2304\)—not just the powers of two \(n_j\)—pick the scale \(j\) with \(n_j\le L\le2n_j\). For even lengths the weights \(p_i=|\lambda_i|^n/Z_n\) lie in \([0,1]\) and sum to one, so raising them to any power \(r\ge1\) can only lower their sum: \(Z_{rn}/Z_n^{\,r}=\sum_ip_i^r\le1\). With \(r=L/n_j\) at \(\beta_j\) this gives \(Z_L(\beta_j)\le Z_{n_j}(\beta_j)^{L/n_j}\), and with base length \(L\) and \(r=2n_j/L\) at \(2\beta_j\) it gives \(Z_L(2\beta_j)\ge Z_{2n_j}(2\beta_j)^{L/(2n_j)}\). Taking their ratio bounds the thermal purity at length \(L\) by \(P_\tau(L,\beta_j)\ge[P_\tau(2n_j,\beta_j)P_{\mathrm s}(n_j,\beta_j)^2]^{L/(2n_j)}\ge(1-\varepsilon_j)^3\ge1-3\varepsilon_j\).

Since \(1-3\varepsilon_j\ge0.97>\tfrac12\), the squares bound \(w_0\ge P_\tau(L,\beta_j)>\tfrac12\) of Section 8 forces a single ground state to hold more than half the total Boltzmann probability, so the ground state is unique. The first excited weight is at most the remaining probability \(1-w_0\le3\varepsilon_j\), so \(e^{-\beta_j\Delta_L}=w_1/w_0\le3\varepsilon_j/(1-3\varepsilon_j)<20^{-2^j}\), and dividing by \(\beta_j=784\cdot2^j\) proves \(\Delta_L>\log20/784\).

The recurrence and the gap deduction are formalized in Lean 4: lean/CertifiedGaps/PurityCriterion.lean.

\[ e^{-\beta_j\Delta_L}=\frac{w_1}{w_0}\le\frac{3\varepsilon_j}{1-3\varepsilon_j}<20^{-2^j}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta_L>\frac{\log20}{784}. \]

17The starting numbers, worked on a toy

Where does the starting purity bound come from? The transfer operator \(X_\beta\) acts on the infinite-dimensional space of bond histories, so a computer can never diagonalize \(X_\beta\) to read off its eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\). What a computer can evaluate exactly are the traces \(Z_k=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,k}=\sum_i\lambda_i^k\)—the partition functions of very small rings. Can a small-ring moment \(Z_6\) together with a lower bound at a larger length \(n=24\) certify the purity \(P_{\mathrm s}(24)=Z_{48}/Z_{24}^2\) without ever seeing the eigenvalues?

Here is the entire logic on a toy example whose true eigenvalues are \(1, 0.6, -0.5, 0.4\), kept hidden from the certificate. First, on a \(6\)-site ring, the computer evaluates the sixth moment \(Z_6=\sum_i\lambda_i^6=1.0664\); since \(\lambda_i^6\ge0\), no single eigenvalue can exceed \(|\lambda_i|\le1.0664^{1/6}=1.011\). Second, to rule out the possibility that \(Z_6\) is spread thinly across many small eigenvalues, we need a lower bound on \(Z_{24}\) at length \(24\). Because \(Z_{24}=\sum_me^{-\beta E_m}\ge e^{-\beta E_0}\) and any trial state \(\psi\) gives a variational upper bound \(E_0\le\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle/\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\), finding a trial state with negative energy (after shifting \(H\) by a constant per site, which cancels in all purities) proves \(Z_{24}\ge1\).

Now combine the upper bound \(Z_6=1.0664\) with the lower bound \(Z_{24}\ge1\). If every eigenvalue obeyed \(|\lambda_i|\le0.97\), each sixth power would obey \(\lambda_i^6\le0.97^6=0.833\). Because \(\lambda_i^{24}=(\lambda_i^6)^4\) and fourth powers are convex, a total sixth-moment mass of \(1.0664\) subject to \(\lambda_i^6\le0.833\) maximizes \(\sum_i\lambda_i^{24}\) when packed into one full bar of \(0.833\) and one remainder bar of \(1.0664-0.833=0.233\), giving at most \(0.833^4+0.233^4=0.484<1\le Z_{24}\)—a contradiction. Thus one dominant eigenvalue \(\lambda_0\) must exceed \(0.97\) (\(\lambda_0^6>0.833\)). The remaining eigenvalues share at most \(0.233\) of the sixth-moment mass, so their twenty-fourth powers sum to at most \(0.233^4=0.0030\). Keeping only \(\lambda_0^{48}\) in the numerator of \(P_{\mathrm s}(24)=\sum\lambda_i^{48}/(\sum\lambda_i^{24})^2\) and bounding the rest of the denominator by \(0.233^4\) gives \(P_{\mathrm s}(24)\ge(1+0.233^4/0.97^{24})^{-2}=0.98765\ldots>0.987\). The real certificate runs these exact three steps with \(12\)-site rings, exact integers, symmetry sectors, and a \(72\)-site trial state.

Toy numbers are exact to the digits shown; the real certificate uses capped masses with degree-12 filters, as in notes/thermal-mpo-gap-certificate.tex.

\[ \sum_i\lambda_i^{24}\le\underbrace{0.833^4+(1.0664-0.833)^4}_{0.484}<1\le Z_{24}\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_0>0.97,\qquad P_{\mathrm s}(24)\ge\Big(1+\frac{0.233^4}{0.97^{24}}\Big)^{-2}=0.98765\ldots>0.987 . \]

18The real certificate

Section 16 took as its starting input that both purities \(P_{\mathrm s}(2304,784)\) and \(P_\tau(4608,784)\) are at least \(0.99\). A ring of \(2304\) spin-1 sites has \(3^{2304}\) states, so its partition function is never computed directly. Instead, OpenAI's certificate runs the three-step toy strategy at a much smaller scale, \((n_0,\beta_0)=(36,12.25)\), and then applies six doubling steps:

  1. Small-ring moments in exact integers: For even ring lengths \(k\le12\) at \(\beta_0=49/4=12.25\), the certificate evaluates the exact partition functions \(Z_k=\operatorname{Tr}X_{\beta_0}^{\,k}\)—along with twisted traces \(Z_k(g)=\operatorname{Tr}(U_gX_{\beta_0}^{\,k})\) that insert a lattice or spin-rotation symmetry \(g\) before closing the ring—in exact integer arithmetic.
  2. Symmetry sectors and spectral filters: The symmetry twists separate the eigenvalues of \(X_{\beta_0}\) by symmetry sector, and degree-\(12\) polynomial filters turn the small-ring traces into upper caps (such as \(|\lambda|\le Z_k^{1/k}\)) on the eigenvalues and moment masses in each sector.
  3. A \(72\)-site matrix-product trial state: To prove \(Z_{72}>1\) without diagonalizing a \(3^{72}\times3^{72}\) matrix, the certificate uses a real matrix-product trial state \(\psi\) on \(72\) sites (one tensor \(A\) per site, with a \(3\)-dimensional spin leg and two \(26\)-dimensional virtual legs joined around the ring). Gluing \(\psi\) to its bra turns the norm \(\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}T^{72}\) and expected energy \(\langle\psi|H_{72}|\psi\rangle\) into traces of \(72\) small matrices, which are contracted exactly. With a per-site energy shift \(\kappa\) (which cancels in all purities), the variational bound \(E_0\le\langle\psi|H_{72}|\psi\rangle/\langle\psi|\psi\rangle<-72\kappa\) proves \(Z_{72}\ge e^{-\beta_0(E_0+72\kappa)}>1\) and forces one dominant eigenvalue \(\lambda_0>0.999\).
  4. Six doublings from length \(36\) to \(2304\): Exactly as in Section 17, \(\lambda_0>0.999\) and the moment caps certify the spatial and thermal defects \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm s},\varepsilon_\tau)=(0.0479,0.1815)\) at length \(36\) and \(\beta_0=12.25\). Six applications of the exact doubling map carry \((36,12.25)\) to \((2304,784)\) and shrink the defects to \((0.0084513,0.0054986)\), both below \(0.01\).

Every number in the spin-1 seed is an integer or an exact fraction. The other models in this repository have shorter correlation lengths, so they diagonalize \(16\)-spin rings completely and bound every floating-point rounding error.

19The whole machine on a solvable chain

Before turning to open chains, we can test the entire bootstrap machine on an exactly solvable spin-\(\tfrac12\) chain where \(Z_n(\beta)\) and the true gap are known in closed form at every size: the transverse-field Ising ring \(H=-\sum Z_jZ_{j+1}-g\sum X_j\), where \(Z_j,X_j\) are the Pauli matrices \(\sigma_j^z,\sigma_j^x\) and \(g\) is the transverse field.

Mapping the spins to free fermions gives \(Z_n(\beta)\) exactly for any even ring length \(n\). Pick a field \(g\), a seed size \(n_0\), and an inverse temperature \(\beta_0\); the page computes both starting defects \((\varepsilon_{\mathrm s},\varepsilon_\tau)\), runs the exact doubling recurrence, and compares the certified gap bound with the true thermodynamic gap \(2|1-g|\). For \(g<1\) the ordered phase has two nearly degenerate ground states, so the thermal purity never approaches \(1\) and every seed fails, as it must. The browser uses standard floating-point arithmetic here, so this interactive laboratory is a diagnostic, not a certificate. At \(g=2\), \((128,32)\) it reproduces the survey value \(1.885\) in AGENTS.md.

Floating point, not a certificate. Free-fermion formulas with the ground-energy mismatch summed by Poisson summation, so tiny defects are not lost to cancellation.

Part II · Open chains

Cut the ring open into a chain with two free ends, and pin the two end spins with a small magnetic field. The same bootstrap, with the open ends of the network closed by boundary vectors instead of a trace, proves that the odd open chains have a unique ground state and a uniform gap [3].
