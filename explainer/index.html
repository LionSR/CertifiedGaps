The spacetime bootstrap

A proof that the spin-1 Heisenberg ring has a spectral gap, in sixteen figures, with an open-chain sequel

A gap is a decay rate: at low temperature, excited states fade like \(e^{-\beta\Delta}\). The proof measures how pure the thermal state is on one finite ring, then shows that doubling the ring and the inverse temperature together makes the purity better by itself, all the way to infinity. Each section states one idea in words, shows it in a figure that runs step by step, and ends with the formula.

1What is a gap?

A spin chain has discrete energy levels. The gap \(\Delta_L\) is the distance from the lowest level to the next one. Haldane predicted in 1983 that the spin-1 chain keeps a gap as the ring grows; numerics give \(\Delta\approx0.41\). For decades there was no proof that some fixed \(c>0\) bounds \(\Delta_L\) for every \(L\).

Haldane, 1983. The theorem here is from OpenAI’s 2026 preprint; this page follows notes/thermal-mpo-gap-note.tex.

\[ H_L=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1},\qquad \Delta_L=E_1(L)-E_0(L). \]

Theorem: \(\Delta_L>\log 20/784\approx0.0038\) for every even \(L\ge2304\), and the ground state is unique.

2A gap is a decay rate

At inverse temperature \(\beta\), level \(m\) has probability \(w_m=e^{-\beta E_m}/Z\). The first two weights have ratio \(e^{-\beta\Delta}\). So it is enough to show that the excited weight decays at least like \(e^{-c\beta}\), with the same \(c\) for every ring. Everything that follows controls excited weight at low temperature on arbitrarily long rings.

\[ \frac{w_1}{w_0}=e^{-\beta\Delta_L}\le e^{-c\beta}\ \text{ for all }\beta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta_L\ge c. \]

3Imaginary time

Expand a state in energy eigenstates, \(\psi=\sum_mc_m|m\rangle\). Real-time evolution multiplies \(c_m\) by the phase \(e^{-iE_mt}\): every probability \(|c_m|^2\) stays the same forever. Put \(t=-i\tau\) and the phase becomes a real factor \(e^{-\tau E_m}\). Nothing rotates any more; each component shrinks, higher energies faster. After rescaling, the ground state wins, and the first excited level loses at rate \(\Delta\). At \(\tau=\beta\) the factors are the Boltzmann weights: "long imaginary time" and "low temperature" are the same statement.

\[ e^{-iHt}\psi=\sum_m c_me^{-iE_mt}|m\rangle\ \xrightarrow{\ t=-i\tau\ }\ e^{-\tau H}\psi=\sum_m c_me^{-\tau E_m}|m\rangle,\qquad \frac{|c_1(\tau)|}{|c_0(\tau)|}\propto e^{-\tau\Delta}. \]

4A sum over histories

One fact does all the work: an entry of a matrix power \(A^M\) is a sum over paths through \(M\) steps, each path weighted by the product of the entries it uses (the same fact that counts walks on a graph). Write \(e^{-\beta H}=A^M\) with \(A=e^{-d\tau H}\) and \(M=\beta/d\tau\). For one spin with \(H=-\sigma^x\), \(A\approx\begin{pmatrix}1&d\tau\\d\tau&1\end{pmatrix}\): staying costs 1, flipping costs \(d\tau\). As \(d\tau\to0\) a path is a world line with a few flips; \(k\) flips can sit anywhere, which gives weight \(\beta^k/k!\). The trace keeps only paths that return to their start, so time is periodic. Summing gives \(2\cosh\beta\), the exact answer. That sum over histories is the Euclidean, or imaginary-time, path integral.

The same identity counts walks on a graph: (Aᴹ)ᵢⱼ with A the adjacency matrix.

\[ (A^M)_{ij}=\sum_{\text{paths }i\to j}\ \prod_{\text{steps}}A_{s_ts_{t+1}},\qquad \operatorname{Tr}e^{\beta\sigma^x}=\sum_{k\ \mathrm{even}}\frac{2\beta^k}{k!}=2\cosh\beta . \]

5The partition function is a torus

Split \(e^{-\beta H}\) into many short steps in imaginary time. The trace glues the last step to the first, so imaginary time is a circle of length \(\beta\). The ring is a circle in space. The partition function is a sum over histories on an \(L\times\beta\) torus. The proof uses an exact continuous-time version of this picture, with no discretization error.

\[ Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H_L}=\operatorname{Tr}\big(e^{-\beta H_L/M}\big)^M . \]

6Histories of a chain

The classical Ising ring has \(Z=\operatorname{Tr}T^L\): a 2×2 matrix passes one spin from site to site. The quantum chain works the same way after one change of what is passed along. Each bond term \(\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}=-\sum_\alpha G_{\alpha,j}G_{\alpha,j+1}\) fires events at random imaginary times, and an event of type \(\alpha\) puts the real 3×3 matrix \(G_\alpha=iS^\alpha\) on both of its sites. The thing passed along the ring is the list of events on a bond. A site with events \(a\) on its left bond and \(b\) on its right contributes \(X(a,b)\): the trace of its \(G\)'s multiplied in time order. Examples: no events gives 3; one \(z\)-event on each side gives \(\operatorname{Tr}G_zG_z=-2\).

Gα = iSα in the Cartesian basis |x⟩, |y⟩, |z⟩ are real antisymmetric 3 × 3 matrices.

\[ X_\beta(a,b)=\operatorname{Tr}\Big[\mathcal T\!\!\prod_{(\tau,\alpha)\in a\cup b}G_\alpha\Big],\qquad Z_L(\beta)=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}\quad\big(\text{cf. }Z=\operatorname{Tr}T^L\big). \]
Try it: compute X(a, b) yourself

Add events in time order. Each event puts \(G_x\), \(G_y\) or \(G_z\) on the site, from the left bond (a) or the right bond (b). X is the trace of the product, later events on the left.

7Two ways to slice the torus

Horizontal slices give the familiar sum over energies. Vertical slices give a transfer matrix \(X_\beta\) that carries one site's whole time history along the ring. A ring of \(L\) sites is \(X_\beta^{\,L}\), traced. The eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) depend on \(\beta\) but not on \(L\). This is the classical Ising transfer matrix, except that each "spin" is now a history in imaginary time.

\[ Z_L(\beta)=\sum_m e^{-\beta E_m}=\operatorname{Tr}X_\beta^{\,L}=\sum_i\lambda_i(\beta)^L . \]

8Purity, seen as squares

Purity measures how close a distribution, or a density matrix, is to a single state. Lay the probabilities along a unit segment and draw a square on each: all squares fit under the tallest one, so the purity is at most the largest probability. A purity of 0.99 means one state holds at least 99%.

\[ P=\sum_ip_i^2\ \le\ p_0\sum_ip_i=p_0 . \]

9Two purities from one torus

Normalize the torus two ways: cut it in space, \(\sigma_n=X^n/Z_n\), or take the Gibbs state \(\rho_n=e^{-\beta H}/Z_n\). The purity of \(\sigma\) glues two tori side by side; the purity of \(\rho\) stacks two. Both are ratios of partition functions, and adding a constant to \(H\) changes neither.

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)=\frac{Z_{2n}(\beta)}{Z_n(\beta)^2},\qquad P_\tau(n,\beta)=\frac{Z_n(2\beta)}{Z_n(\beta)^2}. \]

10Squaring sharpens

Squaring a normalized state doubles the torus in one direction: \(\sigma_n^2=P_{\mathrm s}\,\sigma_{2n}\) and \(\rho_n^2=P_\tau\,\rho_n(2\beta)\). On the probabilities this is \(p_i\to p_i^2/P\). The new defect is at most \(f\) of the old one, about half its square, whenever the old defect is below \(\tfrac12\).

\[ 1-P'\le f(1-P),\qquad f(z)=\frac{z^2}{2(1-z)^2}. \]

11Space and time are tied

At fixed \(\beta\), a longer ring loses purity, so length and \(\beta\) must double together. Gluing four tori into one twice as wide and twice as tall, rows first or columns first, gives one identity. Since every purity is at most 1, it turns a spatial bound at \(\beta\) and a thermal bound at \(2n\) into a spatial bound at \(2\beta\).

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n,2\beta)\,P_\tau(n,\beta)^2=\frac{Z_{2n}(2\beta)}{Z_n(\beta)^4}=P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta)^2\,P_\tau(2n,\beta). \]

12Why the proof needs a symmetric transfer matrix

The squaring step treats \(p_i=\lambda_i^n/Z_n\) as probabilities, so it needs the eigenvalues of the transfer matrix to be real. A two-by-two example makes the danger concrete. For the symmetric matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.3\\0.3&0.5\end{pmatrix}\) the "spatial purity" \(Z_{2n}/Z_n^2\) runs 0.63, 0.83, 0.94, 0.98, … up to 1. For the slightly rotated matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}0.9&-0.4\\0.4&0.9\end{pmatrix}\), whose eigenvalues are complex, the same ratio is 0.40, −0.11, −4.2, −47: no probability can do that, and the argument would collapse.

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n)=\frac{Z_{2n}}{Z_n^2}=\sum_ip_i^2\in(0,1]\quad\text{only if}\quad p_i=\frac{\lambda_i^n}{Z_n}\ \text{are real and}\ \ge0 . \]

13Where the symmetry comes from

Symmetry of \(X\) comes from the bond. In \(X(a,b)\) a site multiplies the matrices of all events in time order, those arriving from its left bond (\(a\)) and from its right bond (\(b\)). For the Heisenberg chain every event inserts the same real matrix \(G_\alpha\) from either side, so the site cannot tell the sides apart: with \(a=\{G_z\ \text{at}\ 0.2,\ G_x\ \text{at}\ 0.6\}\) and \(b=\{G_y\ \text{at}\ 0.4\}\), both \(X(a,b)\) and \(X(b,a)\) equal \(\operatorname{Tr}G_xG_yG_z=1\). A bond that inserts different matrices on its two sides breaks this: with \(G_z\) on the left site and \(P=\mathrm{diag}(1,0,0)\) on the right, two events give 1 from one side and −2 from the other. Reflection positivity is the general condition that the bond looks the same from both sides, \(-h=\sum_kC_k\otimes\Theta(C_k)\), with positive weights.

Formal statement and classification of reflection-positive chains: notes/thermal-mpo-gap-scope.tex, appendices D–F.

\[ -\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}=+\sum_\alpha G_\alpha\otimes G_\alpha\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X(a,b)=X(b,a)\in\mathbb R . \]

14Testing a bond for reflection positivity

To test any bond, regroup the 81 entries of \(-h\) so that rows carry both indices of the left site and columns both indices of the right site. This realignment turns a product \(A\otimes B\) into an outer product \(\mathrm{vec}(A)\,\mathrm{vec}(B)^{\mathsf T}\). A reflection-positive bond becomes a sum of squares \(\sum vv^\dagger\), whose eigenvalues are all \(\ge0\). The spin-1 antiferromagnet gives 2, 2, 2 and zeros; the ferromagnet gives −2, −2, −2 and fails, and no on-site constant can repair it.

Rule for bilinear chains: reflection positive exactly when an even number of couplings Jx, Jy, Jz are ferromagnetic.

\[ R\big[A\otimes B\big]=\mathrm{vec}(A)\,\mathrm{vec}(B)^{\mathsf T},\qquad R\big[-\mathbf S_j\cdot\mathbf S_{j+1}\big]=\sum_\alpha\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)\,\mathrm{vec}(G_\alpha)^{\mathsf T}\succeq0 . \]

15One doubling step

Four moves turn bounds at \((n,\beta)\) into bounds at \((2n,2\beta)\). Moves 1 and 3 use the coupling identity and cost a factor of about 3 in \(\varepsilon\). Moves 2 and 4 square and turn \(3\varepsilon\) into about \(\tfrac92\varepsilon^2\). Net: \(\varepsilon\to5\varepsilon^2\) for \(\varepsilon\le1/100\). The exact map keeps improving as long as \(\varepsilon<\varepsilon_*\approx0.127\).

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(n,\beta),\,P_\tau(2n,\beta)\ge1-\varepsilon\ \Longrightarrow\ P_{\mathrm s}(2n,2\beta),\,P_\tau(4n,2\beta)\ge1-5\varepsilon^2 . \]

16Repeat, and read off the gap

Start from \(\varepsilon_0=1/100\) at \((2304,784)\). After \(j\) steps, \(\varepsilon_j=20^{-2^j}/5\) at \(\beta_j=784\cdot2^j\), which is \(\tfrac15e^{-\beta_j\log20/784}\): exponential decay in \(\beta\). Lengths between doublings follow from the real eigenvalues (\(\ell^p\)-norm monotonicity). The thermal purity then exceeds \(\tfrac12\), the ground state is unique, and the weight ratio gives the gap.

The recurrence and the gap deduction are formalized in Lean 4: lean/CertifiedGaps/PurityCriterion.lean.

\[ e^{-\beta_j\Delta_L}=\frac{w_1}{w_0}\le\frac{3\varepsilon_j}{1-3\varepsilon_j}<20^{-2^j}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta_L>\frac{\log20}{784}. \]

17The starting numbers, worked on a toy

The certificate never sees the eigenvalues of \(X\); it sees exact traces \(Z_n\) of small rings. Here is the whole logic on a toy with hidden eigenvalues 1, 0.6, −0.5, 0.4. The sixth moment \(Z_6=1.0664\) caps every \(|\lambda|\) at 1.011. A trial state gives \(Z_{24}\ge1\); if all \(|\lambda|\le0.97\), the sixth-moment mass could produce at most 0.484, so one eigenvalue must exceed 0.97. The rest then carry at most 0.233 of the mass, which bounds their contribution and gives \(P_{\mathrm s}(24)\ge0.988\). The real certificate runs the same three steps with 12-site rings, exact integers, symmetry sectors and a 72-site trial state.

Toy numbers are exact to the digits shown; the real certificate uses capped masses with degree-12 filters, as in notes/thermal-mpo-gap-certificate.tex.

\[ \sum_i\lambda_i^{24}\le\underbrace{0.833^4+(1.0664-0.833)^4}_{0.484}<1\le Z_{24}\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_0>0.97,\qquad P_{\mathrm s}(24)\ge\Big(1+\frac{0.233^4}{0.97^{24}}\Big)^{-2}=0.988 . \]

18The real certificate

The input is both purities \(\ge0.99\) at \((2304,784)\). A ring of 2304 spin-1 sites has \(3^{2304}\) states, so it is never computed. Instead:

  1. exact integer computation of \(Z_n\) for rings of up to 12 sites;
  2. symmetry and polynomial filters turn these into caps on every transfer eigenvalue;
  3. a 72-site trial state and the variational principle force one dominant eigenvalue;
  4. this gives purities at length 36, and six doublings carry them to 2304.

Every number is an integer or an exact fraction. The other models in this repository diagonalize 16-spin rings completely and bound every floating-point rounding error.

Every number in the spin-1 seed is an integer or an exact fraction: models/spin1/results/scalars.json.

\[ |\lambda|\le Z_k^{1/k}\ (k\ \text{even}),\qquad Z_{72}>1\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_0>0.999, \] \[ (0.0479,\ 0.1815)\ \xrightarrow{\ \times6\ }\ (0.0084513,\ 0.0054986). \]

Part II · Open chains

A chain with two ends and a small field on the end spins. The ring proof carries over with one new ingredient: the ends are closed by boundary vectors instead of a trace, and the proof must show that they overlap the dominant state. Eight figures, following Appendices A–C of the note.

Theorem from OpenAI’s boundary-field preprint; this part follows notes/thermal-mpo-gap-note.tex, Appendices A–C.

The eight open-chain figures as one film, about two and a half minutes.

II.1Ends carry free spins

Opening the ring changes the physics before it changes the proof. In the valence-bond picture each spin 1 is a pair of spin-½, and neighbouring halves bind into singlets; at each end one spin-½ is left over. Exact diagonalization of small chains shows the consequence. Without a field, even chains have three states just above the ground state, at 0.51, 0.31, 0.20, 0.14, 0.10 for n = 4, …, 12, and odd chains have a three-fold degenerate ground state. With a field \(h=3/5\) on the two end spins, odd chains have a single ground state and a gap of 0.72, 0.64, 0.58, 0.55 for n = 5, …, 11.

Levels from floating-point exact diagonalization with conserved Sz (n ≤ 12), a diagnostic and not part of the proof. With an odd number of sites both ends lie on the same sublattice, so equal fields suit both.

\[ H_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_{i+1}-h\,(S_1^z+S_n^z),\qquad h=\tfrac35 . \]

Theorem: for every odd \(n\ge1921\) the ground state is unique and \(\Delta_n>\log10/392\approx0.0059\).

II.2A cylinder, not a torus

The partition function of the open chain is still a trace, so imaginary time is still periodic. Space is not: the chain has two ends. The spacetime is a cylinder, and the network that computes \(Z\) is a row of transfer matrices closed by a vector at each end instead of a trace.

\[ \text{ring: }\ \operatorname{Tr}X^n\qquad\text{open chain: }\ \langle\bar v|\,X^n\,|v\rangle . \]

II.3What a boundary vector is

The end field acts on one site, so expanding \(e^{-\beta H}\) puts field events on that site's world line, each inserting \(G_z=iS^z\). The list of field events is a history, just like the list of events on a bond, and its weight defines a vector on histories: \(v(a)=(ih)^d\) when all \(d\) events are of type \(z\). With field \(-h\) on one end and \(+h\) on the other, the two caps are the same vector, one conjugated, so every even power is a squared norm by the mirror argument of section 12. The equal-field chain of the theorem is reached by a \(\pi\) rotation \(U_P\) on one cap.

The left cap is conjugated: its events carry (−ih)Gz = +hSz, adding −hS1z; the right cap adds +hSnz.

\[ v_\beta(a)=(ih)^d\ \ (\text{all labels }z),\qquad \|v_\beta\|^2=e^{\beta h^2},\qquad Y^{\mathrm{same}}_n=\langle\bar v|X^nU_P|v\rangle . \]

II.4The overlap

A trace always contains the top eigenvector of the transfer matrix; a boundary vector might not. Split the normalized transfer matrix into its top eigenvector and the rest, \(T=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|+R\) with \(\|R\|<1\). Repeated application keeps the component of \(v\) along \(\psi\) and shrinks the rest, so the open-chain network splits into a fixed overlap \(c\) and a remainder that dies with length. The boundary defect \(e\) measures the remainder's share; any \(e<1\) proves \(c>0\).

\[ B_m=\langle v|T^m|v\rangle=\underbrace{|\langle\psi,v\rangle|^2}_{c}+\underbrace{\langle v|R^m|v\rangle}_{D_m},\qquad e_m=\frac{D_m}{B_m}=1-\frac{c}{B_m}. \]

II.5Why the remainder is a square

For even \(m\) the remainder folds in half: the two halves of the strip are mirror images, so \(D_m\) is a squared norm. Absorbing \(k/2\) factors into each end leaves a middle of norm at most \(\|R\|^{m-k}\), which shrinks the remainder geometrically while the overlap \(c\) is unchanged. This is how a longer chain improves the boundary defect.

\[ D_m=\big\|R^{m/2}v\big\|^2\ge0,\qquad D_m\le\|R\|^{m-k}D_k . \]

II.6Three moves per scale

The open-chain bootstrap tracks two numbers per scale: the boundary defect and the thermal purity of the open chain at length \(M_j\). Move 1 uses the geometric suppression at the next temperature to squeeze the boundary defect. Move 2 doubles the length, which costs little once the overlap is controlled. Move 3 squares the Gibbs state. One step sends a defect \(x\) to \(10x^2\).

Composition in imaginary time links the scales: Bm(j+1) = Pτopp(m, βj) rj−m Bm(j)2.

\[ e_{M_j/2}(j)\le x,\ \ P_\tau^{\mathrm{opp}}(M_j,\beta_j)\ge1-x\ \Longrightarrow\ e_{M_j}(j{+}1)\le10x^2,\ \ P_\tau^{\mathrm{opp}}(2M_j,\beta_{j+1})\ge1-10x^2 . \]

II.8Starting numbers

The iteration starts at \(\beta=49\) from four certified numbers: periodic purities at length 120 and 240, and two open-chain bounds from the boundary-field paper. Three rounds bring both defects to 0.001 at \(\beta=392\), length 1920. From there \(x\to10x^2\) gives \(x_j=10^{-1-2^{j-5}}\).

Rows j = 2…5 are the rational table of the note; recomputing the recurrence gives values at or below each entry.

\[ P_{\mathrm s}(120,49),\,P_\tau(240,49)\ge0.948,\quad e_{142}\le0.14,\quad P_\tau^{\mathrm{opp}}(240,49)\ge0.79 . \]

II.9Odd lengths, and the gap

For odd \(n\) the remainder \(R^n\) need not be positive. Putting \(M/2\) factors on each end leaves a middle \(R^{n-M}U_P\) of norm at most 1, so the remainder is bounded by \(D_M\) whatever its sign, and the rotation \(U_P\) acts trivially next to the top eigenvector. The windows \([M_j,2M_j]\) cover every \(n\ge1920\); in window \(j\) the thermal purity is at least \(1-4x_j>\tfrac12\), the ground state is unique, and the weight ratio gives the gap.

\[ e^{-\beta_j\Delta_n}\le\frac{4x_j}{1-4x_j}<10^{-2^{j-5}},\quad\beta_j=392\cdot2^{j-5}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta_n>\frac{\log10}{392}. \]
